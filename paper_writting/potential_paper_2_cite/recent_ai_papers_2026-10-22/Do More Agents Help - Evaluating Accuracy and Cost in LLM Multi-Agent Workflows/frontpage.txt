Abstract

Recent studies examine how workflow structure affects performance and computational cost. Xu et al. [34] compare homogeneous
workflows with single-LLM executions that retain their workflow
logic. Kim et al. [18] compare canonical coordination architectures across interactive tasks and model families. We complement
these studies by comparing workflow designs normalized to a common execution substrate: Jarvis, LLM-Debate, EvoAgent, AutoGen,
CAMEL, and ChatEval, each compared with a common controller,
BenchAgent Core. Across ten fixed task sets, we report gains, regressions, and token expenditure, then use retained responses to
identify concrete directions for improving these configurations.
BenchAgent provides common benchmark inputs, model settings, tool interfaces, answer contracts, scoring rules, and token
accounting across workflows. We evaluate BenchAgent Core, a
single task-level controller, and six MAS configurations on ten
benchmarks spanning mathematical and general reasoning, reading comprehension, coding, instruction following, and retrieval.
The primary study uses GPT-4.1 and three runs on fixed task sets,
with 3,302 instances per configuration per run. We also report
single-run GAIA results and auxiliary backend checks, along with
a separate case study of an external runtime.
Figure 1 summarizes the comparison protocol. We report accuracy and token use for individual benchmarks and the pooled suite,
using BenchAgent Core as the single-agent reference throughout.
The study makes three contributions:
A shared framework for workflow comparison. BenchAgent
places normalized multi-agent implementations and a single-agent
controller under common task, tool, answer, scoring, and accounting interfaces. This supports consistent measurement of complete
configurations and provides a common basis for evaluating subsequent workflow changes.
Broad evidence across seven workflows and ten benchmarks.
We compare six MAS configurations against BenchAgent Core over
three runs, quantifying benchmark-specific gains, regressions, and
pooled accuracy–token trade-offs. The resulting profiles show how
each workflow’s task strengths relate to its aggregate performance
and cost, providing evidence for workflow selection and further
optimization.
Targeted evidence for workflow optimization. Analyses of 371
parseable ChatEval GSM8K tasks and 163 matched Jarvis–ChatEval

Choosing whether to replace a single-agent controller requires evidence on both task performance and inference expenditure. We
present BenchAgent, a shared evaluation framework, and compare
six multi-agent workflow configurations against its single-agent
reference, BenchAgent Core, across ten reasoning, coding, and tooluse benchmarks. All configurations use GPT-4.1 and common tool
and scoring interfaces; Core executions share the same step limits. We measure accuracy and recorded token use over three runs
on fixed task sets, with 3,302 instances per configuration per run.
EvoAgent reaches 79.70% mean pooled accuracy versus 76.65% for
Core and exceeds Core in all three runs; the other five MAS trail
by 1.71–8.76 percentage points. LLM-Debate gains 9.67 points on
HumanEval but loses 1.96 points in pooled accuracy while consuming 1.39 times the tokens of Core. ChatEval gains 22.25 points
on IFEval but trails Core in pooled accuracy with 4.34 times its
token consumption. The broad comparison and targeted analyses
of retained responses provide evidence for workflow selection and
identify answer preservation and resource allocation as directions
for subsequent multi-agent optimization.

Introduction

Multi-agent workflows offer several ways to organize the capabilities of a language model: debate candidate answers, exchange
role-specific messages, delegate tool use, or evolve agent instructions [11, 20, 31, 33, 37]. A workflow may improve accuracy on one
benchmark yet perform worse across a broader task suite. Even
a higher aggregate score can hide regressions on particular tasks.
Workflows with similar accuracy may also differ in token consumption. We examine benchmark-level results alongside pooled
accuracy and recorded token use to identify where replacing a
single-agent controller brings gains, where it incurs costs, and
which behaviors merit further optimization.
