Within Example Trial 1 (Table 6.2.2 and Figures 6.2.1-6.2.2), there are 18 deaths on the
placebo arm (15%). Results are shown for 11-29 total deaths on the treatment arm with
mortality hazard ratios from the mortality component alone ranging from 0.68 to 1.58.
Simulations representative of rows highlighted in Table 6.2.2 are presented in Figures
6.2.1-6.2.2. The treatment effect estimated from the functional component alone for those
participants who did not experience mortality range from 0.71-0.73. As the number of
deaths increases on the active treatment arm, the posterior estimate for the shared
treatment effect between mortality and function from the Bayesian shared-parameter
model increases (0.73 to 0.88) and the posterior probability that the DRR is less than one
decreases (0.994 to 0.853). Similarly, the CAFS one-sided p-value increases (0.002 to
0.233). For all mortality hazard ratios less than or equal to one, both CAFS and the Bayesian
shared-parameter model indicate a significant treatment benefit. For the Bayesian shared-
parameter model, the tipping point at which results become non-significant occurs
between mortality hazard ratios of 1.04 and 1.10 (17 to 18 deaths in the active treatment
arm). Under the CAFS analysis, the tipping point at which results become non-significant
occurs between mortality hazard ratios of 1.15 to 1.27 (19 to 21 deaths on the active
treatment arm). Under the simulated conditions, the Bayesian shared-parameter model
was more sensitive to a discrepancy between the effect of treatment on function and
mortality than the CAFS analysis.
Within Example Trial 2 (Table 6.2.3), there are 11 deaths on the placebo arm (9%). Results
are shown for 6-19 total deaths on the treatment arm with mortality hazard ratios from the
mortality component alone ranging from 0.66 to 1.57. The treatment effect estimated from
the functional component alone for those participants who did not experience mortality
ranges from 0.72-0.75. As the number of deaths increases on the active treatment arm, the
posterior estimate for the shared treatment effect between mortality and function from the
Bayesian shared-parameter model increases (0.71 to 0.85) and the posterior probability
that the DRR is less than one decreases (0.998 to 0.917). Similarly, the CAFS one-sided p-
value increases (0.001 to 0.085). For all mortality hazard ratios less than or equal to 1.13
both CAFS and the Bayesian shared-parameter model indicate a significant treatment
benefit (CAFS p-value less than .025 and Bayesian posterior greater than 0.979). For the
Bayesian shared-parameter model, the tipping point at which results become non-
significant occurs between mortality hazard ratios of 1.13 to 1.21 (11 to 12 deaths on the
active treatment arm). Under the CAFS analysis, the tipping point at which results become
non-significant occurs between mortality hazard ratios of 1.27 to 1.33 (13 to 14 deaths on
the active treatment arm). As in Example Trial 1, the Bayesian shared-parameter model
was more sensitive to a discrepancy between the effect of treatment on function and
mortality than the CAFS analysis. We also note that with the lower mortality rate, both the
Bayesian shared-parameter analysis and the CAFS analysis were less sensitive to a
discrepancy between the effect of treatment on function and mortality.
