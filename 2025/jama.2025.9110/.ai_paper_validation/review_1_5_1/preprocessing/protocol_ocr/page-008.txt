246 — Observational data in favour of augmenting protein delivery
247 Prospective and retrospective observational studies have reported that augmenting enteral protein
248 (protein delivered at > 1.2 g/kg/day) is associated with reduced mortality, shorter time to discharge
249 alive, reduced duration of mechanical ventilation, and reduced duration of ICU and hospital
250 admission”"?, However, the impact of unmeasured confounders in such observational data may be
251 substantial. Nonetheless, based solely on observational data, international critical care nutrition
252 guidelines recommend delivery of dietary protein of > 1.2 g/kg body weight/day'* '*. Due to the poor
253 quality of available evidence these recommendations are graded as ‘consensus only’ or ‘very low
254 — quality’.

256 Observational data against augmenting protein delivery
257 Whilst most observational studies suggest benefit with more protein, there are also observational data
258 that greater protein delivery can cause harm. A major cohort study from the UK published in JAMA
259 reported a greater loss of quadriceps muscle — diagnosed on ultrasound — in those that received more
260 dietary protein'’. In addition, in a pre-planned observational study of 1440 critically ill children within
261 a large randomised clinical trial of nutritional therapy, increased protein was associated with worse
262 outcomes, e.g., increased protein increased the risk of infections and reduced the likelihood of being
263 liberated from mechanical ventilation and discharged alive from ICU".
