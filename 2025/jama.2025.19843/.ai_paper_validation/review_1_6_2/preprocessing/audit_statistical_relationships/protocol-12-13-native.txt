2.3 
Why recent large large randomized controlled trials  of levosimendan were all 
negative? 
Levosimendan has been tested in large RCTs against dobutamine or placebo in patients with 
decompensated heart failure, septic shock and the low cardiac output syndrome after cardiac surgery.  
 
In patients with acute decompensated heart failure, while a first proof of concept trial suggested 
that levosimendan was associated with improved haemodynamic performance and lower mortality 
than dobutamine [15], the large (n=1,327) randomized double-blind SURVIVE clinical trial failed to 
confirm these findings [17]. In this trial, patients received a loading dose of levosimendan (12 μg/kg) 
followed by an infusion of 0.1 up-to 0.2 μg/kg/min for an additional 23 hours, as tolerated. Although a 
greater decrease in B-type natriuretic peptide level was observed in the first 5 days of treatment in the 
levosimendan group, all-cause mortality at 180 days was similar between groups, respectively 
occurring in 26% in the levosimendan group and 28% in the dobutamine group (HR 0.91; [0.74-1.13]; 
p=0.40). Marked hypotension, probably related to the levosimendan loading dose, atrial fibrillation, 
and hypokalemia were more frequent in the levosimendan group, possibly explaining the negative 
results of this trial.  
 
The Levosimendan for the Prevention of Acute Organ Dysfunction in Sepsis (LeoPARDS) trial 
tested whether the addition of levosimendan to standard care would reduce the severity of organ 
dysfunction among patients with septic shock [18]. The administration of levosimendan or placebo 
was started at a rate of 0.1 μg/kg/min, increased to 0.2 μg/kg/min for a further 20 to 22 hours in the 
absence of rate-limiting side effects. The addition of levosimendan to standard treatment was not 
associated with less severe organ dysfunction or lower mortality. The mean (±SD) SOFA score over the 
ICU stay was 6.68±3.96 in the levosimendan group and 6.06±3.89 in the placebo group (P = 0.053) and 
mortality at 28 days was 34.5% in the levosimendan group and 30.9% in the placebo group (P = 0.43). 
More patients in the levosimendan group than in the placebo group had supraventricular 
tachyarrhythmia, tachycardia and hypotension. The negative results of this trial may relate to the 
inclusion of very few patients with septic shock and confirmed myocardial dysfunction and to the 
administration of relatively high dose of levosimendan (up to 0.2 μg/kg/min), which induced marked 
tachycardia and hypotension, the vasodilating properties of drug possibly aggravating sepsis-induced 
vasoplegia in this setting.  
 
Meta-analyses of small trials [19] suggested that levosimendan may result in a higher rate of 
survival among patients at risk of developing or presenting the low cardiac output syndrome after 
cardiac surgery and set the stage for 3 large RCTs testing the drug in this setting. The LICORN [20] 
(n=336 patients) and the LEVO-CTS [21] (n=882 patients) trials assessed the ability of preoperative 
levosimendan to prevent postoperative low cardiac output syndrome in patients with pre-operative 
left ventricular dysfunction. The continuous prophylactic infusion of 0.1 μg/kg/min was started just 
after anesthetic induction and was maintained over 24 hours in the absence of serious adverse 
reactions. The occurrence of the composite end-points of prolonged catecholamine infusion, use of 
left ventricular mechanical assist device, or renal replacement therapy (LICORN) and of death, renal-
replacement therapy, perioperative myocardial infarction, or use of a mechanical cardiac assist device 
(LEVO-CTS) and the rates of adverse events were not different between levosimendan and placebo 
treated patients in these studies. It should be mentioned that only 22% of the patients developed low 
cardiac output syndrome in the LEVO-CTS trial and that in both the LICORN and the LEVO-CTS trials, 
only half of the patients needed catecholamine support for >24 hours. Thus, the preventive use of 
levosimendan does not seem to confer clinical benefits in this setting. Alternatively, the CHEETAH trial 
[22] included only patients who developed perioperative or early post-operative cardiovascular 
dysfunction and low cardiac output syndrome, defined as the need for support with an intraaortic 
balloon pump or high-dose inotropic support in patients with preoperative left ventricular ejection 
fraction of less than 25%. Patients were randomly assigned to receive levosimendan at a dose of 0.025 
to 0.2 μg/kg/min or placebo, with no loading dose, for up to 48 hours or until discharge from the 
intensive care unit. The trial was stopped early for futility after 506 patients were enrolled and there

2.4 
Rationale for a trial testing levosimendan in VA-ECMO patients and justification for the choice 
of subjects 
Preliminary retrospective cohort studies suggested a benefit of the early administration of 
levosimendan in refractory cardiogenic shock patients on VA-ECMO. In a small “before-after” study of 
medical cardiogenic shock patients, Affronti et al. [23] showed that the drug was associated with a 
higher rate of VA-ECMO weaning compared to historical controls, respectively 83.3% vs 27.3% 
(p=0.498). Similarly, Tafer et al [24] reported more ECMO-free days (22 vs 9) and lower 30-day 
mortality (24% vs 56%, p<0.05) in a cohort of 80 patients of whom 37 had received levosimendan early 
after ECMO initiation. In a series of 240 patients undergoing VA-ECMO after cardio-vascular surgery, 
of whom 75% received levosimendan immediately after ECMO initiation [25], the treatment was also 
associated with higher rates of successful ECMO weaning [HR; 0.41; 95% CI, 0.22–0.80; P=0.008]. 
Additionally, lower 30-day (adjusted HR 0.52; 95% CI 0.30–0.89; P=0.016), and long-term survival 
(adjusted HR 0.64; 95% CI 0.42–0.98; P=0.04) were reported, although levosimendan-treated patients 
had higher baseline EuroSCORE (P<0.001) and SAPS-3 (P=0.02) and lower LVEF (p=0.04).  
 
Indeed, the rationale for the early use of levosimendan after VA-ECMO initiation appears strong 
in patients with refractory cardiogenic shock related to conditions such as acute myocardial infarction, 
myocarditis, post-cardiac surgery or post-cardiac arrest. Myocardial injuries in these situations share 
many common pathophysiological features, including ischemia, inflammation and increased oxidative 
stress leading to extensive myocardial stunning and dysfunction [26-28]. Besides its inotropic 
properties that might quickly improve myocardial contractility, levosimendan might also exert 
beneficial antiinflammatory, antioxidative, antiapoptotic, cardioprotective and anti-stunning effects 
[29-35] that might accelerate cardiac recovery allowing earlier weaning from the support. 
Furthermore, it might be possible to administer higher doses of levosimendan to this specific group of 
patients, since VA-ECMO also provides robust peripheral vascular support that might protect against 
vasoplegia and hypotension that were suspected to hamper the benefit of the drug in previous trials.  
 
In conclusion, the probability of demonstrating lower mortality with levosimendan in 
cardiogenic shock patients on ECMO appears unlikely based on the results of recent large RCTs. 
However, any medical therapy that may be able to hasten myocardial recovery and shorten the 
duration of VA-ECMO support might translate in significantly less morbidity and ultimately reduced 
ICU and hospital length of stays and associated costs. Highly relevant pathophysiological and clinical 
reasons indicate that levosimendan might fulfill these objectives. A multicenter randomized trial with 
sufficient statistical power is therefore urgently needed to test the early administration of 
Levosimendan to facilitate and accelerate VA-ECMO weaning in refractory cardiogenic shock patients 
supported by VA-ECMO.
