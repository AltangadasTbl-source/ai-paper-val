SUMMARY
Rationale: It is uncertain what the optimal ventilation strategy is in intensive
care unit (ICU) patients without the Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome
(ARDS): ventilation with a lower tidal volume and higher respiratory rate or
ventilation with a higher tidal volume and lower respiratory rate.
Hypothesis: Ventilation with a lower tidal volume and a higher respiratory
rate increases the number of ventilator–free days and alive of patients without
ARDS at onset of ventilation. As such, lower tidal volume ventilation shortens
ICU length of stay and can be expected to reduce healthcare costs.
Objective: The present study aims to compare a ventilation strategy with a
lower tidal volume and a higher respiratory rate with ventilation using a higher
tidal volume and a lower respiratory rate in patients without ARDS at start of
ventilation.
Study design: This is a national multicenter randomized controlled trial in
intubated and ventilated ICU patients without ARDS, according to the Berlin
definition.
Study population: Consecutive patients admitted to the ICUs of the
participating centers without ARDS and an anticipated duration of ventilation
of more than 24 hours.
Procedure: Patients are randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to lower tidal
volume ventilation (4 to 6 ml/kg predicted body weight) and a higher
respiratory rate, the ’lower tidal volume’–arm, or higher tidal volume ventilation
(8 to 10 ml/kg predicted body weight) and a lower respiratory rate, the ‘higher
tidal volume’–arm. Respiratory rates are adjusted to reach a blood pH of 7.25
to 7.45.
Study endpoints: The primary endpoint is the number of ventilator–free days
and alive at day 28 after ICU admission. Secondary endpoints include ICU –
and hospital length of stay (LOS) and – mortality (follow up till day 90);
incidence of development of ARDS, pneumonia, atelectasis, and
pneumothorax; cumulative use and duration of sedatives, and neuromuscular
blocking agents; incidences of ICU delirium and ICU acquired weakness;
patient–ventilator asynchrony; and the need for decreasing of instrumental
dead space. Finally, health care costs will be estimated from a health

NL47013.018.14

PReVENT

systems perspective over the time horizon of the RCT. Incremental cost per
ICU day avoided is the primary cost–effectiveness endpoint.
Nature and extent of the burden and risks associated with participation,
benefit and group relatedness: Burden and risks of the two ventilation
strategies are uncertain. Lower tidal volume ventilation could induce an
increased need for sedation and increase the risk of asynchrony and
respiratory muscle fatigue, while ventilation with higher tidal volumes could
increase the risk of lung injury. Both could lead to a longer duration of
ventilation. Collection of data causes no harm for the patients.

1. INTRODUCTION AND RATIONALE
Mechanical ventilation associated injury
Mechanical ventilation is generally seen as an invasive but safe supportive
strategy in critically ill patients with respiratory failure. However, there is
unequivocal evidence from both experimental and clinical studies that
ventilation has a strong potential to aggravate and even initiate injury to the
lungs [1, 2] and the respiratory muscles [3, 4]. Ventilation results invariably in
a pattern of ventral overstretching and dorsal collapse of lung tissue, which
both play a role in development of so–called ‘ventilator–induced lung injury’
(VILI). Ventilation is associated with respiratory muscle disuse and even
misuse causing atrophy of diaphragmatic myofibers, which play a role in
development of so–called ‘ventilator–induced diaphragm dysfunction’ (VIDD)
[5].
Ventilation with lower tidal volumes in patients with ARDS
The harmful effects of the traditional use of a higher tidal volume were not
recognized until 2000 when the beneficial effects of ventilation with a lower
tidal volume (6 ml/kg predicted body weight, PBW) in patients with the Acute
Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) were established in the landmark
NHLBI ARDS Network trial [6]. Some clinicians and investigators were slow to
accept these findings, but subsequent trials and a meta–analysis convincingly
confirmed the reduction in mortality by using lung-protective ventilator settings
in patients with ARDS [7]. Currently, lung protective ventilation is considered
standard of care for ARDS (i.e., moderate or severe ARDS see paragraph
1.4) [8–11].
Uncertainty over the use of lower tidal volumes in patients without
ARDS
It is uncertain whether we should also use a lower tidal volume in patients
without ARDS. One meta–analysis suggested that ventilation with a lower
tidal volume benefits patients without ARDS [12]. The meta–analyzed studies,
however, all had methodological shortcomings and the majority of them
focused on ventilation during general anesthesia for surgery, which cannot be
simply generalized to other clinical situations like ventilation of critically ill
patients in the ICU setting. There are several arguments against use of lower
tidal volume ventilation in these patients: one argument is that a

compensatory higher respiratory rate could increase sedation needs [13],
which could increase the incidence of ICU delirium [14] and ICU acquired

risk of patient–ventilator asynchrony [17, 18]; more effort can induce more
pendelluft and thereby lung injury [19], finally, an alleged side-effect of lower
tidal volume ventilation is patient fatigue although there are no studies to
support this. All of these could offset the beneficial effects of lower tidal
volume ventilation as found in patients with ARDS. Thus, it is uncertain
whether ventilation with lower tidal volume should be used routinely in all ICU
patients. Consequently its use is not yet recommended in guidelines for
ventilation of patients without ARDS resulting in remarkable and unwanted
practice variation and continued use of higher tidal volumes [20, 21], as
illustrated in figure 1 that shows the modus of tidal volume size in recent

Need for a new randomized controlled trial of ventilation in patients
without ARDS
The ICU community explicitly requests a well–powered trial comparing
ventilation with a lower tidal volume to ventilation with a higher tidal volume in
patients without ARDS [12, 26]. This trial should use patient–relevant and only
objective outcomes, including duration of ventilation and ICU– and hospital
length of stay (LOS), amongst other outcomes. The ‘PRotective VENTilation
in patients without ARDS at start of ventilation’–trial (PReVENT) is the first
randomized controlled trial comparing lower tidal volume ventilation with
higher tidal volume ventilation in patients without ARDS at onset of ventilation
that recruits a sufficient number of patients to test the hypothesis that lower
tidal volume ventilation benefits patients without ARDS at start of ventilation
with regard to clinically relevant endpoints. Second, PReVENT is the first
prospective study that will evaluate the incremental cost–effectiveness of
lower tidal volume ventilation in patients without ARDS.

2. OBJECTIVES AND HYPOTHESIS
Objectives
2.1.1 Primary objective
The aim of PReVENT is to compare a ventilation strategy using lower tidal
volumes and higher respiratory rates with a ventilation strategy using higher
tidal volumes and a lower respiratory rate in intubated and ventilated intensive
care unit (ICU) patients without Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)
at start of ventilation.
2.1.2 Secondary objectives
Secondary objectives are to investigate the effects of tidal volume size on
ICU– and hospital LOS and – mortality, the incidence of development of
ARDS, pneumonia, atelectasis, and pneumothorax, the cumulative use and
duration of sedatives, and neuromuscular blocking agents, incidences of ICU
delirium and ICU acquired weakness, patient–ventilator asynchrony and the
need for decreasing of instrumental dead space.
Hypothesis
2.2.1 Primary hypothesis
We hypothesize ventilation with lower tidal volumes to benefit patients without
ARDS at start of ventilation by increasing the number of days alive and free
from ventilation at day 28. As such using a lower tidal volume instead of a
higher tidal volume is expected to be a cost–effective modification of
ventilation in patients without ARDS.
2.2.2 Secondary hypotheses
We hypothesize ventilation with lower tidal volumes to reduce ICU – and
hospital LOS and – mortality, the incidence of development of ARDS,
pneumonia, atelectasis, and pneumothorax, the cumulative use and duration
of sedatives, and neuromuscular blocking agents, incidences of ICU delirium
and ICU acquired weakness, patient–ventilator asynchrony and the need for
decreasing of instrumental dead space.

3. STUDY DESIGN
PReVENT is an investigator–initiated, national, multicenter, parallel
randomized controlled two–arm trial in intubated and ventilated intensive care
unit patients without Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome at start of
ventilation. A cost–effectiveness analysis will be performed alongside the trial.

4. STUDY POPULATION
Population
We will recruit consecutive intensive care unit (ICU) patients who do not meet
the consensus clinical definition for Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome
(ARDS) at onset of ventilation and who need invasive ventilation for an
anticipated duration of > 24 hours. Patients are recruited in the ICUs of five
centers in the Netherlands; three academic centers, and two teaching
hospitals. Patients are screened for eligibility and randomized within an hour
of initiation of invasive ventilation or, if already intubated and ventilated before
admission, on admission to the ICU. We will randomize a total of 952 patients.
It is expected that each participating center will randomize approximately 200
patients.
Inclusion criteria
In order to be eligible to participate in this trial, patients must meet all of the
following criteria:
•

Admission to an ICU participating in this trial

•

Need for intubation and ventilation

•

Within 1 hour of admission from the operation room or emergency room (if
still intubated and ventilated), or within 1 hour of start of invasive
ventilation in the ICU

•

An expected duration of ventilation > 24 hours
Exclusion criteria

•

Age less than 18 years

•

Patients previously randomized in PReVENT

•

Patients participating in other interventional trials

•

Patients with a clinical diagnosis of ARDS or possible ARDS with a
PaO2/FiO2 < 200 mm Hg (for reasons of safety, see insert below)

•

Invasive ventilation longer than 12 hours directly preceding admission

•

Patients with suspected or confirmed pregnancy

•

Patients with increased and uncontrollable intracranial pressure (of ≥18
mmHg)

•

Patients with GOLD classification III or IV chronic obstructive pulmonary
disease (COPD)

•

Patients with asthmatic status

•

Patients with premorbid restrictive pulmonary disease (evidence of chronic

•

Patients with any previous pneumectomy or lobectomy
The diagnosis of ARDS is clinical, requiring (a) a medical history, (b) the
presence of bilateral opacities on the chest X–ray that are not fully explained
by effusions, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules, and (c) respiratory failure not
fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload. The PaO2/FiO2 is used to
classify ARDS severity, with a PaO2/FiO2 between 200 and 300 mm Hg
indicating mild ARDS, and a PaO2/FiO2 < 200 mm Hg indicating moderate or
severe ARDS.
This diagnostic approach, however, could be difficult if not impossible in ICU
patients within the first hour after intubation and start of ventilation: they
frequently suffer from temporary post–intubation atelectasis as a reason for
a low PaO2/FiO2, the medical history is often not yet complete, and imaging
studies are usually not yet performed or the results available. The risk is that
only the PaO2/FiO2 is used to diagnose ARDS in the short time frame after
intubation, which could induce severe bias, as many of these patients do not
have ARDS.
Thus, we exclude all patients that are clinically diagnosed with ARDS.
Patients with a PaO2/FiO2 < 200 mm Hg are also excluded since we consider
these patients at high risk of having ARDS; only when the attending physician
explicitly states the patient has no ARDS and no direct risk factor for ARDS
is present, the patient can be included. Patients without ARDS and with a
PaO2/FiO2 between 200 and 300 mm Hg can be included: as these patients
very seldom have ARDS.

Sample size
The required sample size is calculated using data from the recently published
meta–analysis [12] and a secondary analysis of this meta–analysis using
individual patient data from the studies performed in ICU patients [27]. The
sample size is computed on the basis of the hypothesis that ventilation with

NL47013.018.14

PReVENT

lower tidal volumes is associated with a reduction of one day of ventilation. A
sample size of 397 patients in each group has 80% statistical power to detect
a difference of one ventilator–free day and alive at day 28 after ICU
admission, with means of 23 and 24 days respectively. Assuming that the
common standard deviation is 5 using a two group t–test with a 0.05 two–
sided significance level. The sample size is increased by 20% to correct for
dropouts and lost to follow up meaning that each group will contain 476
patients.

5. TREATMENT OF SUBJECTS
Treatment
Patients randomized to the ‘lower tidal volume’–arm are ventilated with a tidal
volume of 4 to 6 ml/kg predicted body weight (PBW); patients randomized to
the ‘higher tidal volume’–arm are ventilated with a tidal volume of 8 to 10
ml/kg PBW. Patients are randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the ‘lower tidal
volume’–arm or the ‘higher tidal volume’–arm.
Ventilation protocol
The allowed ventilation modes are volume controlled ventilation and pressure
support ventilation (i.e., pressure controlled ventilation, combined modes
volume controlled and pressure controlled ventilation, and automated modes
of ventilation are NOT allowed). The inspiration–to–expiration ratio with
volume controlled ventilation is 1:2. With volume controlled ventilation the
inspiration time and pause are set at 25% and 10% respectively. With
pressure support ventilation the highest possible pressure rise is chosen, and
cycling off is set at 25%.
The inspired oxygen fraction is 0.21 or higher to maintain oxygen
saturation 90 to 92% and/or PaO2 > 7.3 to 10.7 kPa (55 to 80 mmHg). The
respiratory rate is adjusted to maintain a blood pH of 7.25 to 7.45. In case of
metabolic acidosis or – alkalosis, a lower or higher than normal PaCO2 can be
accepted, left to the discretion of the attending physician. The lowest level of
positive end–expiratory pressure is 5 cmH2O; allowed FiO2–PEEP–
combinations are provided in table 1. Recruitment maneuvers are allowed,
when deemed necessary, left to the discretion of the attending physician.
In both arms a tidal volume is titrated per PBW, which is calculated
according to a previously used formula [6]: 50 + 0.91 x (centimeters of height
– 152.4) for males and 45.5 + 0.91 x (centimeters of height – 152.4) for
females.
5.2.1 Lower tidal volume ventilation
Patients randomized to the ‘lower tidal volume’–arm start with a tidal volume
of 6 ml/kg PBW. The tidal volume size is decreased in steps of 1 ml/kg PBW
per hour, to a minimum of 4 ml/kg PBW, unless the patient suffers from
severe dyspnea (identified by increased respiratory rate > 35 breaths per
minute accompanied by increasing levels of discomfort with or without need

titration is by variation of the pressure support level. Other modes of
ventilation are not allowed.
Patients randomized to the higher tidal volume arm generally need
more support when the ventilator is switched to pressure support ventilation,
but the maximal airway pressure should not exceed 25 cm H2O [2]. In case
the resulting tidal volume remains below 10 ml/kg PBW this must be accepted
(i.e., this is neither a reason to use (more) sedation and/or muscle relaxants,
nor to switch the ventilator to volume–controlled ventilation) (see weaning).
Other modes of ventilation are not allowed.

Sedation protocol
Sedation follows the local guidelines for sedation in each participating units. In
general, these guidelines favor the use of analgo–sedation over hypno–
sedation, use of bolus over continuous infusion of sedating agents, and the
use of sedation scores.
Nurses determine the level of sedation at least 3 times per day. The
adequacy of sedation in each patient is evaluated using a Richmond Agitation
Sedation Scale (RASS) [29, 30]. A RASS score of –2 to 0 is seen as
adequate sedation. As stated above, sedation adjustments should never be
done to allow a lower or higher tidal volume. The goals of sedation are to
reduce agitation, stress and fear; to reduce oxygen consumption (heart rate,
blood pressure and minute volume are measured continuously); and to
reduce physical resistance to– and fear of daily care and medical
examination. Patient comfort is the primary goal.
Level of pain is determined using scales such as Numeric Rating Scale
(NRS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Critical Care Pain Observation Tool
(CCPOT) or Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS).

Study procedures
Patients in participating intensive care units (ICU) are screened and
randomized within 1 hour of start of mechanical ventilation in the unit.
Demographic data on screened patients regardless of meeting enrollment
criteria will be recorded (registry: age, gender, type of surgery).
Randomization, blinding and treatment allocation
Randomization will be performed using a dedicated, password protected,
SSL–encrypted website. Randomization sequence is generated by a
dedicated computer randomization software program using random block
sizes and is stratified per center and per intubation location (i.e., in the ICU or
before ICU admittance in the operation room or in the emergency room). No
blocking is applied to other trial factors. Due to the nature of the treatment,
blinding is not possible.
Patients are randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to lower tidal volume
ventilation (4 to 6 ml/kg PBW) (the ‘lower tidal volume’–arm) or ventilation with
higher tidal volumes (8 to 10 ml/kg PBW) (the ‘higher tidal volume’–arm).

8. STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Primary study parameter(s)
The primary outcome, the number of ventilator–free days and alive at day 28
after ICU admission, is analyzed using Cox’s regression. Possible imbalance
between groups will be modeled in the Cox model. P-values of 0.05 are used
for statistical significance. When appropriate, statistical uncertainty will be
expressed by the 95% confidence levels. All statistical analyses will be
performed with the R language and environment for statistical computing.
Secondary study parameter(s)
Continuous normally distributed variables will be expressed by their mean and
standard deviation or when not normally distributed as medians and their
interquartile ranges. Categorical variables will be expressed as n (%). To test
groups Student’s t test will be used, if continuous data is not normally
distributed the Mann-Whitney U test will be used. Categorical variables will be
compared with the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact tests. Time dependent
data will be analyzed using a proportional hazard model adjusted for possible
imbalances of patients’ baseline characteristics. Analysis will be performed
with R statistics version 3.0.2. Patient characteristics will be compared and
described by appropriate statistics.
Primary and secondary analyses
The goal of the primary analysis is to quantify the effect of lower tidal volumes
vs. higher tidal volumes on the number of ventilator free days and alive at day
28. Statistical analysis will be based on the intention–to–treat principle. We
will also perform a per–protocol analysis, comparing patients who received
lower tidal volumes and patients who received higher tidal volumes. Other
secondary analyses include analysis of patients who had pneumonia versus
patients without pneumonia,, and patients with sepsis versus patients without
sepsis.

COST–EFFECTIVENESS ANALYSIS
Cost–effectiveness endpoints
The primary cost-effective (CE) measure will be incremental cost per ICU day
avoided; the secondary CE measure is incremental cost per mechanical
ventilation day avoided.
Perspective on costs and time horizon of the analysis
Costs will be considered from a health systems perspective over the time
horizon of the analysis. As this time horizon is shorter than 1 year, discounting
of future costs and effects is not applicable.
Collection of resource use and cost data
Collection and analysis of cost data is fully integrated into the study. Analyses
of effect parameters are guided by the above described analysis plan and
hypotheses. Also in accordance with the guideline, analyses are conducted
with an intention-to-treat approach.
Per patient volumes of resource use associated with mechanical
ventilation, stay in ICU, stay in hospital (other wards), and cumulative use of
sedatives, neuromuscular blocking agents, tracheostomies, and ventilator–
associated pneumonia, will be collected from the hospitals’ administration
databases. Unit prices for each cost parameter will be established using the
hospitals’ financial data, where available, or national tariffs as recommended
in the Costing Guideline published by the Dutch Health Insurance Council
(CVZ) [39]. Costs are calculated as volume * unit price.
Analysis and presentation of Incremental Cost–effectiveness
Ratios
Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratios (ICER) will be calculated by dividing
the difference in costs between the two ventilation strategies by the difference
in effect between the two ventilation strategies. Cost data do not conform to
the assumptions for standard statistical tests for comparing differences in
arithmetic means, i.e. they are usually right-skewed and always truncated at
zero, Therefore the nonparametric bootstrap is the appropriate method to
compare means and calculate 95% confidence intervals around the ICER,
and analyse the probability of the ventilation strategy with low tidal volume
being cost-effective. Bootstrapping is performed using Excel® software

NL47013.018.14

PReVENT

(Microsoft, Redmond, WA) Results are presented on the cost–effectiveness
graph, plotting the incremental costs against the incremental effectiveness.

Sensitivity Analyses
One and multi-way sensitivity analyses will be applied to test the robustness
of the ICER against parameter uncertainty, by varying the parameter inputs
over a predefined range of alternative inputs. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis
(PSA), using 10.000 second order Monte-Carlo simulations, will be employed
to handle uncertainty in the cost-effectiveness analysis by obtaining
distributions of costs, effects and the resulting cost-effectiveness estimates.
For this, distributions will be fitted to the effect and cost parameters using the
method of moments approach, i.e. equating the mean and SE observed in the
trial data to the expressions for the mean and SE of the appropriate
distribution. The parameters of the distribution (α, β) can then be solved
analytically. Gamma distributions will be fitted to costs parameters. Normal or
lognormal distributions will be fitted to the effect parameters, depending on
the observed distribution of these data. Sensitivity analysis will be performed
in combined Excel® and R software.
Subgroup analyses
As we expect variation in cost structures between academic and non–
academic ICUs and hospitals, all analyses will also be performed for these
subgroups using mean cost data specific to academic and non–academic
centres.
Analysis of missing data
In case of >10% missing data, these will be imputed using the SPSS (IBM,
Armonk, NY) multiple imputation algorithm, to reflect the uncertainty that is
inherent when replacing missing data.

9. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
Regulation statement
This study will be conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of
Helsinki as stated in the current version of Fortaleza, Brazil, 2013 and in
accordance with the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO).
Recruitment and consent
9.2.1

Deferred consent

For this study we ask for deferred consent and we appeal to the emergency
procedure for consent in medical research as stated in article 6, paragraph 4
of the WMO because of the following reasons.
Mechanical ventilation is one of the most often applied therapies in the
ICU. Patients who are in respiratory distress are in an emergency situation
and need immediate ventilator support, start of ventilation cannot be
postponed. Patients admitted for ventilation to an Intensive Care Unit (ICU)
are, without exception, incompetent to give informed consent and obtaining
informed consent from a legal representative takes on average up to half a
day, even by an experienced research team.
We propose to randomize each patient who meets the inclusion criteria
ultimately within 1 hour after ICU admission (if ventilation started before
admission), or within 1 hour after intubation and start of ventilation (if
ventilation started after admission). Ventilation according to randomization is
started immediately thenceforth. Informed consent from the legal
representative will be requested as soon as possible thereafter, but never
later than 24 hours after randomization. If informed consent is not obtained
within those 24 hours, or if a legal representative denies participation within
this time frame, the patient is excluded and data will no longer be used.
Thenceforth the patient is ventilated according to the policy of the attending
physician.
9.2.2

Injurious effects of ventilation

Ventilation can harm the lungs and respiratory muscles, even after a short
period of ventilation. If a patient, in the proposed trial, is already ventilated for
several hours, effects of ventilation could already be in place, largely reducing
validity of the trial outcomes.
From experimental animal studies we know that mechanical ventilation

NL47013.018.14

PReVENT

can cause effects within an hour of ventilation with a higher tidal volume [40].
These findings are in line with results from clinical studies, showing ventilatorrelated effects after relative short periods of ventilation, e.g. after ventilation
during general anesthesia for surgery [41]. Two recently published
randomized controlled trials of patients undergoing major surgery, comparing
a ventilation strategy including use of a lower tidal volume with a ventilation
strategy including use of a higher tidal volume, showed an important effect of
ventilation on the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications [42,
43].
9.2.3

Time between asking for and obtaining informed consent

Most critically ill patients who need ventilation cannot be approached for
informed consent for a study at ICU admission. Indeed, those patients are
usually in severe respiratory distress, sedated or in coma. A prospective
observational study on study recruitment practices in critically ill patients
performed by a respected and experienced research group in Canada [44]
showed that time from recognizing study eligibility to obtaining informed
consent by a legal representative was as high as 12 hours, even while time
from recognition to the first contact with a legal representative was as short as
2 hours. This is in line with our own experience. In a recent observational
study in the Academic Medical Center, Amsterdam, the Netherlands, time
from recognizing of study eligibility to obtaining informed consent from a legal
representative was as high as 14 hours (study ID NL34294.018.10). These
findings are quite understandable if we realize that relatives of a severely ill
patient do not oversee the situation in the circumstances of emergency,
rendering them incapable of making a balanced decision [44–46].
The experience of ICU patients enrolled under deferred consent is
mainly positive. To investigate contentment of patients that were included
using deferred consent, a questionnaire was designed for– and distributed
under the participants of the large NICE–SUGAR trial [47], a trial that
compared a strict blood glucose control strategy with one that accepts higher
blood glucose levels [48]. Of the responders (79% of all participants), a large
majority (96%) said to have granted consent if they would have been asked. A
large majority (93%) mentioned they were happy with the decision made by
the representative at the moment they were incapable of giving informed

NL47013.018.14

PReVENT

consent [48].
9.2.4

Ethical aspects

We can underpin the idea of ‘clinical equipoise’ [49]. Both ventilation
strategies are used interchangeably in ICUs worldwide and are considered
safe. We do not know what the best ventilation strategy is. The most recent
observational study of ventilation practice in ICU patients shows that the
median tidal volume size is 7.5 [6.1 to 8.7] ml/kg predicted body weight (PBW)
[20]. This is in line with a recent meta–analysis of four observational studies of
patients without the Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) in the
United States, Finland and the Netherlands between 2007- 2013 [22–25], that
shows the used tidal volume size is 8 ml/kg PBW [yet unpublished data]. This
meta–analysis also showed there is large variability in tidal volume size, as
illustrated in figure 1. Notably, 11% of patients is ventilated with a tidal volume
of 4 to 6 ml/kg PBW, and 53% is ventilated with a tidal volume of 8 to 10 ml/kg
PBW; the two strategies in the proposed trial.
9.2.5

Similarities with previous studies using deferred consent

The proposed trial has many similarities with recently published interventional
trials in ICU patients using deferred consent [47, 50–53]. In a French trial in
ICU patients with hypovolemic shock who needed immediate resuscitation
with intravenous fluids, colloid infusion was compared with crystalloid infusion
[50]. An international ICU study compared resuscitation with 4% albumin
infusion with normal saline infusion [51]. In the RENAL replacement therapy
study in critically ill patients with acute kidney injury, continuous venovenous
hemodiafiltration with either a lower or higher intensity was compared [52].
One Dutch trial in ICU patients with hypovolemic shock compared lactate–
guided fluid resuscitation with standard resuscitation [53]. In these trials, alike
in the proposed trial, patients were incapable at the moment therapy had to be
started, the effects of the interventions were for a major part depending on its
start, and the strategies under study were both used in daily practice.
9.2.6

Conclusions

Critically ill patients in need of ventilation are, without exception, incapable at
the moment of ICU admission. Obtaining informed consent from a legal
representative takes many hours. Start of ventilation in patients who need
ventilatory support cannot wait. Both ventilation strategies are used

NL47013.018.14

PReVENT

interchangeably in ICUs worldwide and are considered safe.
Benefits and risks assessment, group relatedness
Burden and risks of the two ventilation strategies are uncertain. Lower tidal
volume ventilation could induce an increased need for sedation and increase
the risk of asynchrony and respiratory muscle fatigue, while ventilation with
higher tidal volumes could increase the risk of lung injury. Both strategies are
currently used; there is no additional risk for patients enrolled in this study
compared to standard care.
We specifically chose not to exclude incompetent patients for two
reasons. First, most if not all severely ill patients needing mechanical
ventilation should be considered incompetent due to their needs for
continuous sedation. Second, the strategies to be compared in this study are
to be used in severely ill, intubated and ventilated patients. These conditions
are not present in patients who are not suffering from a critical disease. We
therefore consider it impossible not to include these patients in a study
comparing strategies for mechanical ventilation to be used in that specific
patient group.
Compensation for injury

10.ADMINISTRATIVE ASPECTS, MONITORING AND PUBLICATION
Handling and storage of data and documents
All patients will be addressed to with a random patient identification code. The
codebook will be stored digitally and in paper. The paper version will be
stored behind a lock and the digital form will be encrypted with a double
password. All data will be stored for the length of the study and for 15 years
afterwards, for further publication. All handling of personal data will comply
with the Dutch Personal Data Protection Act.
Monitoring and Quality Assurance
Queries on the database will be done by a statistician and analyzed by the
monitor to signalize early aberrant patterns, trends, issues with consistency or
credibility and other anomalies. On-site monitoring will comprise controlling
presence and completeness of the research dossier and the informed consent
forms, source data checks will be performed as described in the monitoring
plan. Every participating center will be visited at least once every year. The
monitor of this study is: A.L.I.P. van der Veen (Academic Medical Center,
University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). The details of
monitoring are described in the monitoring plan.
Amendments
Amendments are changes made to the research after a favorable opinion by
the accredited METC has been given. All amendments will be notified to the
METC that gave a favorable opinion. All substantial amendments will be
notified to the METC and to the competent authority. Non-substantial
amendments will not be notified to the accredited METC and the competent
authority, but will be recorded and filed by the sponsor.
Annual progress report
The sponsor/investigator will submit a summary of the progress of the trial to
the accredited METC once a year. Information will be provided on the date of
inclusion of the first subject, numbers of subjects included and numbers of
subjects that have completed the trial, unexpected problems and
amendments.
End of study report
The investigator will notify the accredited METC of the end of the study within
a period of 8 weeks. The end of the study is defined as the 90th day after the
