41   Abstract
42   Very few commonly used neonatal surgical therapies have been evaluated to determine their safety.
43   Premature infants are especially vulnerable to unrecognized treatment hazards. Inguinal hernia (IH) repair is
44   the most commonly performed major operation in premature infants, is associated with a 30-40% morbidity
45   rate, and it is unknown whether the operation should occur prior to neonatal intensive care unit (NICU)
46   discharge or be delayed. Early IH repair likely increases anesthesia and cardiorespiratory serious adverse
47   events (SAEs), whereas delayed IH repair likely increases hernia-related adverse events. A growing concern
48   is the possibility that early anesthesia exposure impairs neurodevelopment. The randomized clinical trial (RCT)
49   proposed will be the first trial to evaluate the effect of timing and duration of anesthesia exposure on
50   neurodevelopmental outcomes. Our objective is to conduct a multicenter RCT to determine whether early
51   inguinal hernia repair (prior to NICU discharge) or late inguinal hernia repair (~5 months after NICU discharge)
52   is the safer surgical approach for premature infants who are diagnosed with an inguinal hernia while in the
53   NICU. Assuming a 60% randomization rate, which is supported from a feasibility pilot RCT, 615 infants will be
54   randomly assigned to early or late IH repair over a 2.5-year period. The primary hypothesis is that late IH
55   repair is safer as defined by a 10% absolute reduction in the proportion of infants with >1 SAE (relative risk =
56   0.66; number needed to harm of 10 for early repair) within 10 months after enrollment. The primary outcome,
57   as adjudicated by a committee masked to treatment group assignment, is the proportion of infants with >1 SAE
58   during the study period. Secondary outcome measures include the number of hospital days during the study
59   period (hypothesis: median 3 days less with late IH repair) and, in selected centers (n=200 infants),
60   neurodevelopmental testing with the Bayley Scales of Infant Development (BSID), 3rd edition, at 2 years
61   corrected age (hypothesis: 0.4SD higher Cognitive Composite score with late IH repair). The potential
62   differences in treatment effect according to prespecified patient characteristics (at enrollment) will be examined
63   by frequentist and conservative Bayesian methods and a method to predict the relative safety of early and late
64   IH report for individual infants will be developed and tested. The trial is utilizing the Pediatric Surgery
65   Research Collaborative (PedSRC), a recently established cooperative group committed to performing
66   prospective clinical studies in pediatric surgery. Successful completion of the RCT proposed will stimulate
67   future studies within pediatric surgery.
68
69   Statement of Problem
70   The problem we are addressing in the HIP Trial is the lack of knowledge regarding whether IH repair in
71   premature infants who are diagnosed while in the NICU is more safely performed, for the majority of infants,
72   prior to discharge (early repair) or ~5 months after NICU discharge (late repair). Additionally, the timing (and
73   duration) of general anesthesia is believed to be an important determinant of neurodevelopmental outcomes
74   beyond infancy. The HIP trial will also compare neurodevelopmental outcomes, as assessed by the Bayley
75   Scales of Infant Development, 3rd edition (BSIDIII), at 2 years corrected age, for infants in the early versus late
76   IH repair groups.
77
78   Hypotheses
79   Primary: Late IH repair (5 months after NICU discharge, ~ 55-60 weeks post-menstrual age [PMA]) in
80   premature neonates with an IH will result in a 10% absolute reduction in the proportion of infants with > 1 SAE
81   compared to early IH repair (prior to NICU discharge, ~ 38 weeks PMA).
82
83   Secondary:
84       Late IH repair will be associated with a 0.4 SD increase in mean scores on the BSIDIII Cognitive
85         Composite score (6 points absolute difference) compared to early IH repair.
86       Despite an overall treatment effect favoring late IH repair, the actual treatment effect will vary for pre-
87         defined and specified patient subgroups.
88       Successful conduct of this RCT, as defined by >60% randomization and achievement of required
89         sample size, will further establish the Ped SRC as an entity that will promote further conduct of
90         prospective clinical trials within pediatric surgery and foster successful grant submissions by junior and
91         established pediatric surgeons.

 96   Specific Aims
 97   Primary Aim: Compare the overall safety of early and late IH repair with respect to significant adverse events
 98   (SAEs) and total hospital days in the 10 months after randomization.
 99
100   The primary outcome measure is the proportion of infants with >1 SAE, measured from enrollment through
101   10 months after enrollment. Final determination of SAE will be by an independent adjudication committee,
102   masked to treatment group assignment, consisting of an experienced pediatric surgeon, a neonatologist, and
103   others, at their discretion.
104
105   Secondary outcome measures will include the number of hospital days from enrollment through 10 months
106   beyond enrollment. This measure was chosen because it is easily measured, objective, and is a direct
107   measure of the impact of underlying SAEs. With regard to this outcome, we hypothesize that late IH repair will
108   be associated with a 3 day reduction in the median total number of hospital days during this period (18 hospital
109   days for early IH repair versus 15 for late IH repair). Additionally, participants will be asked to return to
110   Vanderbilt’s neurodevelopmental clinic for a neurodevelopmental follow-up exam when they are 22-26 months
111   corrected age (chronological age - # weeks/months premature). A standard neurological exam and the Bayley
112   Scales of Infant Development, 3rd Edition (BSIDIII), will be administered by a trained examiner at this follow-up
113   visit. The BSIDIII is a standardized assessment and was chosen for its validity, reliability, ease of
114   administration, and widespread use for the targeted age group across facilities.
115
116   Secondary Aims:
117      1. Compare the overall safety of early and late IH repair with respect to neurodevelopmental outcome at
118         22-26 months corrected age (at selected sites with well-established follow up programs).
119      2. Using Bayesian and frequentist analyses, evaluate specific hypotheses about patient characteristics
120         that may influence the relative safety of early and late IH repair.
121      3. To conduct the HIP Trial in an exemplary manner and thereby promote the newly established pediatric
122         Surgery Research Collaborative as an ongoing research network.

125   Rationale / justification
126   Lack Of Neonatal Surgery Safety Studies. The overall problem we are addressing is the near total lack of well-
127   designed research evaluating the safety of neonatal surgical therapies. In part due to the lack of evidence,
128   current decision-making regarding neonatal surgery relies primarily on the preferences of surgeons and
129   neonatologists. Although robust efforts have been made in many fields to assess and improve patient safety
130   since the Institute of Medicine report “To Err is Human”, the same cannot be said for neonatal surgery.1-2 There
131   is a critical void in our knowledge regarding the safety of even the most common neonatal surgical therapies. 3-4
132   Decades of experience in neonatal medicine demonstrate the dangers of using therapies without rigorous
133   testing of their short and longer-term safety.5-11 This knowledge gap in one of the most vulnerable of all patient
134   populations is unacceptable and is the focus of our proposal.
135
136   Timing Of Premature Inguinal Hernia Repair. We have chosen to focus on IH repair in premature infants
137   because: 1) It is the most commonly performed major operation in premature infants 12,13; 2) The morbidity rate
138   is 30-40% in this vulnerable population14-17; 3) Current care varies and is nearly equally split between early IH
139   repair (prior to NICU discharge, at ~38 weeks PMA) and late IH repair (5 months after NICU discharge, at ~
140   55-60 weeks PMA)18,19; 4) Physician treatment preferences are not insurmountable (unlike with some surgical
141   treatments), the recruited surgeons are willing to randomize infants to receive either therapy, and parental
142   consent would be much more easily obtained than in an RCT of emergency surgical procedures in unstable
143   infants; and 5) A RCT of IH repair in premature infants offers a unique opportunity to study the effect of timing
144   of general anesthesia on neurodevelopmental outcomes. The American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on
145   Fetus and Newborn and the Section on Surgery recently highlighted the surgical care of premature infants with
146   IH as problematic, associated with high morbidity and variable care, and needing multicenter randomized trials
147   to compare the treatment options.20

149   Unlike IH repair in term infants or older children, IH repair in premature infants is a technically demanding
150   operation with many potential operative and anesthetic risks. Early IH repair is associated with a high risk of
151   cardiorespiratory and anesthetic adverse events (AEs) and potentially an increased risk of intra operative
152   technical AEs (e.g. injury to vas deferens, gonadal vessels, intestine, or other structures). 21-24 However, the
153   longer IH repair is deferred, the longer the infant is exposed to potential hernia-related AEs (e.g. incarceration,
154   strangulation, unplanned readmission, emergency operation), although cardiorespiratory and anesthetic AEs
155   are likely decreased.25-26 The controversial clinical question involving this surgical choice (early v. late IH
156   repair) is almost entirely focused on safety, rather than effectiveness.20 Despite many retrospective case
157   series, there has never been a multicenter RCT to adequately address the very fundamental question of
158   whether IH repair should be performed prior to NICU discharge or should be deferred until a later time in
159   premature infants. There is a large gap in current knowledge regarding the relative safety of each surgical
160   approach.20
161
162   The RCT we propose--the HIP Trial (Hernia in Premies Trial)--is a true safety study with the goal of measuring
163   and reducing overall serious adverse events, “resulting from a medical intervention, or lack thereof”. Measuring
164   and reducing safety, or harm, is difficult although there are multiple methods available. 27 We will measure
165   serious adverse events (SAEs), defined as an unintended injury or complication caused by health care
166   management resulting in an escalation of care, disability at discharge, death, prolonged stay in hospital or a
167   subsequent admission to the hospital. Health care management includes the decisions and actions of
168   individual providers (e.g. whether to perform IH repair prior to NICU discharge, or withhold such treatment until
169   later) in addition to the broader systems and process of care. These definitions are evidence-based and
170   adapted from existing safety literature.28,29 Although patient safety definitions vary, the universal goal of any
171   patient safety effort is to minimize the incidence and impact of, and maximize recovery from, adverse events.30
172   “Patients who fail to receive needed treatments, or who are subjected to the risks of unneeded care, are placed
173   at risk for injury every bit as objectionable as direct harm from a surgical mishap.” 1 In addition to carefully
174   measuring important patient-centered safety outcomes, we are doing this in the context of a RCT, which is
175   uncommon and highly desirable within the safety discipline. Adverse outcomes that are caused by the
176   treatment decisions and actions of individual providers are best distinguished from those resulting from the
177   underlying patient problems by using the study design we propose: a pragmatic RCT. If one surgical strategy
178   is shown to be associated with fewer SAEs (as hypothesized), the pragmatic RCT design also offers the best
179   chance of changing provider behavior towards adopting the safest approach.31
180
181   Anesthesia Risk. The importance of the HIP trial relates partly to the serious and growing concern that
182   anesthesia during infancy32,33 may have long-term adverse effects on the developing brain, particularly in
183   premature infants who have other insults.34 The vast majority of premature infants undergoing IH repair have
184   general anesthesia. Multiple studies have identified an association between general anesthetic and
185   neurotoxicity35-39, however, none of these studies were randomized clinical trials and have serious limitations
186   as such. The bulk of information on the detrimental effects of anesthesia exposure comes from animal models
187   and the duration of anesthesia exposure in these studies is typically in excess of what is typical in pediatric
188   surgical procedures33. Inguinal hernia repair is considered the standard and required treatment for infants with
189   an inguinal hernia. Anesthesia (general, regional, or both) is a necessary component of this surgical treatment..
190   Further study is critical to define the window of vulnerability, identify the extent of anesthesia-induced neuronal
191   alterations and the functional end points, and assess how to avoid the potential adverse effects. 40 The FDA’s
192   Anesthetic and Life Support Drugs Advisory Committee concluded that the evidence was strong enough to
193   forego elective procedures in children less than 3 years old.33 This report also goes on to say that due to a lack
194   of sufficient evidence, an association between anesthesia exposure and neurodevelopmental disabilities
195   cannot be made. Furthermore, the FDA urges the scientific and medical communities to produce additional
196   research, specifically randomized clinical trials, to fully understand what implications, if any, result from infant
197   exposure to anesthesia.
198   Whether general anesthesia causes adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes is very difficult to determine in
199   humans, in part because of multiple confounders in observational studies comparing exposed and unexposed
200   children. There is only one relevant ongoing RCT (and none previously). 41 In this trial (GAS Trial,
201   Clinicaltrials.gov NCT00756600), infants with an IH are randomized to either general or regional anesthesia.
202   The primary outcome is the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence full scale IQ score at 5
      HIP Trial Protocol
                                                                                                                            5
203   years. Limitations of this trial in assessing effects on patient safety is that it may not be very generalizable to
204   institutions that have limited expertise with regional anesthesia in infants and SAEs that may occur with
205   regional but not general anesthesia (e.g., intraoperative adverse events with patient movement under regional
206   anesthesia only) are apparently not being measured.
207
208   Our proposal addresses what we regard to be a fundamental flaw in the current research agenda related to the
209   risk of anesthesia in infants -- the lack of surgeon involvement and leadership. Surgeons are key decision
210   makers regarding the necessity of interventions that require anesthesia and the timing of these procedures.
211   Early IH repair is likely to entail not only earlier exposure but also longer anesthesia times (due to increased
212   technical difficulty). The HIP trial offers a unique opportunity to evaluate what parents and providers care about
213   most, i.e. the combined, “real world” effects of surgery and anesthesia on the outcomes of vulnerable
214   premature infants, to be identified in a large pragmatic RCT that avoids the confounding that plagues
215   observational studies.

254   Study design. The trial is a multicenter randomized pragmatic clinical trial comparing the safety of early versus
255   late IH repair in premature infants who are diagnosed with an IH while in the NICU, or other nursery, at a
256   participating site.89 Randomization will be stratified according to site and GA strata (<28 weeks and >28 weeks
257   estimated GA) to further ensure as equal risk groups as possible.
258
259   Patient recruitment and educational component.         Based on prior experience with the Necrotizing
260   Enterocolitis Surgery Trial (NEST), we understand that randomization of surgical therapies is difficult for
261   parents and providers. SUBJECT LOCATOR will be used as a recruitment tool to identify potential research
262   subjects based on data available in VU clinical systems (e.g. STAR Panel, WizOrder, Clinic Scheduling). The
263   SUBJECT LOCATOR program is part of a toolset available through VICTR that enables teams to specify
264   inclusion/exclusion criteria for a specific study. The inclusion/exclusion criteria are codified for computable use
265   and combined with data coming through VU Clinical Systems to proactively identify individuals who might
266   qualify for a study. Once a 'match' is made, research study personnel are alerted using confidential messaging
267   or a secure web portal and they may then use the information to further review the patient’s information using
268   the Vanderbilt Electronic Medical Record. If the patient is further deemed a candidate for the study, the study
269   personnel will notify the patient’s care provider who will then ask the patient if they would be interested in
270   communicating with study personnel about research population. We will encourage at least one educational
271   visit to the parents of each eligible infant to discuss the risks and benefits associated with early and late IH
272   repair, the importance of the trial, and the requirements of participation. After parents have received baseline
273   information about the risks associated with an IH and its treatment, they will be better informed prior to
274   deciding about participation in the HIP trial. The primary goal of this strategy is to promote patient recruitment,
275   adherence to the protocol, and full understanding of the rationale for the trial. This has been used successfully
276   in prior pediatric trials.90 We expect >60% of eligible patients to be successfully randomized, based on the pilot
277   trial and another similar trial conducted by Dr. Blakely.14,42 Participating sites were carefully screened and
      HIP Trial Protocol

278   selected to ensure surgeon willingness to randomize patients. Prior performance of trials and willingness to
279   randomize infants in the NEST trial were important considerations. We are using conservative estimates of

294   CONSORT ensures proper and consistent reporting of RCTs and that the sample enrolled is an accurate
295   representation of the population as a whole. To better understand the patient population, anonymous, de-
296   identified information will be housed in the REDCap database and will include:
297         The number of patients screened for the study
298         The gestational age and gender of screened infants
299         The number of eligible patients
300         For those ineligible, the reason(s) why
301         For those eligible but not enrolled, the reason(s) why and the planned treatment (early or late IH repair)
302   This de-identified, anonymous data will demonstrate whether or not the randomized cohort is similar or
303   different to the overall pool of participants. Thus, we will be able to determine whether or not the resulting
304   findings of the RCT are generalizable. The coordinator and PI at each site will communicate regularly with their
305   institution’s surgeons, neonatologists, research support personnel, residents, fellows, nurses and surgery
306   schedulers to ensure they are aware of babies that present with an inguinal hernia so that all applicable infants
307   are screened for possible participation.
308
309   Enrollment and Randomization. Every effort will be made to obtain signed informed consent by the
310   parent(s)/guardian(s) as soon as possible. When the surgeon or neonatologist determines the baby may be a
311   good candidate for the HIP trial but parent/guardian is unavailable for written consent, phone consent will be
312   obtained. The research coordinator or PI will provide the parent with the study information and answer any
313   questions the parents have. If parental consent is given over the phone, the baby will be randomized to
314   treatment group so that providers can determine the treatment plan for the participant. The consent process
315   will continue when the parent is again at the hospital where written consent will be obtained. Written consent
316   will be obtained no later than the day of surgery for the IH repair. After obtaining informed consent, research
317   coordinators will create a new patient record in the REDCap database, enter patient data, and engage the
318   REDCap randomization module. This easy to use interface will provide an immediate treatment group
319   assignment, which will be stratified according to site and GA strata. Enrollment will occur when the infant is
320   approximately 2 weeks from anticipated NICU discharge. This is the exact time at which the decision is
321   currently made (outside any study) whether to perform the IH repair or defer until later.

335   Study population. During the 2.5-year enrollment period, 615 patients will be randomized to either early or late
336   IH repair, after obtaining informed consent from the parent or legal guardian. Data from all eligible patients will
337   be entered into the REDCap database as well as their enrollment status. For infants that were not
338   randomized, the reasons for this will be recorded (e.g. parent or provider refusal, or other reasons).

340             Inclusion Criteria: 1) GA < 37 wks, 0 days; 2) In a NICU / other nursery, at participating sites; 3)
341             Diagnosed with an IH per the pediatric surgeon; and 4) Parental consent and providers willing to
342             randomize the infant.
343
344             Exclusion Criteria:

354   Treatment Groups.
355   a. Early IH Repair = IH repair is performed promptly after randomization and prior to NICU discharge; ~38
356   weeks PMA, based on pilot trial and experience in practice.
357
358   b. Late IH Repair = IH repair will be performed at a goal of 5 months after NICU discharge (~ 55 - 60 weeks
359   PMA). This PMA was selected as the goal time for late repair because this reflects current surgical care and
360   most Children’s Hospitals consider allowing the operation to be done as an outpatient at this time. The decision
361   regarding admission will be determined by the providers for each infant according to site guidelines.

363   A small percentage of infants in either treatment group will likely undergo IH repair at times other than the goal
364   times, due to clinical considerations. For example, some infants assigned to late IH repair will undergo repair
365   prior to 55-60 weeks PMA due to hernia incarceration. These occurrences are important outcome measures

369    Study Procedures and Outcome Determination.
370    After treatment group assignment, each infant will receive care according to the practice at each site, in
371    keeping with a pragmatic trial design. Existing treatment practices that will be allowed include different
372    operative approaches (laparoscopic or open IH repair), anesthesia management (general or regional or other),
373    and other treatment considerations. All details will be documented for analysis. There are no prior studies
374    indicating an impact on the HIP trial outcome measures based on these differences in treatment.
375
376    Data will be collected from these broad temporal categories: demographics, perinatal, NICU course prior to
377    enrollment, surgical consult just prior to enrollment, status of infant at enrollment, information at NICU
378    discharge, intraoperative details (includes surgery and anesthesia-specific data), outpatient data and data at
379    the conclusion of the first phase of the trial (10 months after randomization). Ten months was selected as the
380    end of SAE and hospital days determination by assuming that the earliest PMA at NICU discharge would be at
381    35 weeks. An infant discharged at 35 weeks PMA who had very late IH repair at 65 weeks PMA would still
382    have a 1.5 - 2 month post-operative observation period. The aim is to have the same observation period for
383    infants in both treatment groups to decrease ascertainment bias. Infants in the early IH repair group will have
384    a longer observation period postoperatively, whereas those in the late IH repair group have a longer
385    observation period preoperatively. This study design represents clinical practice and will allow the detection of
386    SAE related to performing or delaying IH repair, which is our goal. After the first phase of the study (10 months
387    after enrollment), surveillance for SAEs will cease and the patient will then enter the “tracking phase” in
388    preparation for neurodevelopmental assessment at 22-26 months corrected age (chronological age - # weeks /
389    months premature).

392    Ascertainment of Primary Outcome (proportion of infants with >1 SAE in the 10 months after enrollment).

456   compared to 20% in the late repair group 293 infants per group (N = 586 total) will be required to have 80%

471    The data will be analyzed using intent-to-treat analyses. Primary and secondary outcomes will be analyzed
472   using general linear mixed models (GLMMs) with both continuous and categorical outcomes as dependent
473   variables. We will examine all variables to determine that they are relatively symmetric and unimodal, examine
474   group variances for obvious deviations, and examine residuals from each model to ensure reasonable
475   adherence to the assumptions of the models. We will evaluate the comparability of our groups on key
476   demographic variables and will adjust for them if necessary (e.g., gender, ethnicity).
477
478     For the primary outcome of the % of infants with >1 SAE, we will use a GLMM (binomial with log link) with
479   treatment group (early, late) and GA (<28; >28 wks) stratum as covariates and center as a random effect.
480   Hospital days will be analyzed with a lognormal model using treatment group and GA as covariates (center as
481   a random effect). A linear mixed model will be used for BSIDIII scores with same covariates and random
482   center. The absolute risk difference (which is better understood than relative risk by clinicians and patients)
483   and 95% confidence interval (CI) will be reported for the primary outcome. For continuous outcomes, we will
484   report absolute differences and 95% CIs. Similar models will be used to analyze all other secondary outcomes.
485
486    In addition to the planned final frequentist analysis, we will also conduct a final Bayesian analysis of the
487   primary outcome and major secondary outcomes. All analyses will be conducted using neutral priors centered
488   at relative risk of 1.0 with 95% credible interval of 0.33-3.0.
489
490    Treatment Effect Modifiers. To investigate treatment effect modifiers for the primary and secondary outcomes
491   (hospital days and BSIDIII scores), we will use Bayesian hierarchical models with terms of interaction between
492   treatment group (early, late) and the prespecified potential moderators: GA (<28; >28 wks) bronchopulmonary
493   dysplasia (yes/no), maternal education <12 yrs (yes/no), prenatal care (yes/no), IH difficult to reduce (yes/no).
494   The conservative Bayesian approach of Dixon and Simon99 allows us to specify a priori how likely (or unlikely)
495   it is for subgroup differences to be present and to shrink the subgroup estimates to the overall mean treatment
496   effect. Prior distributions for main effects will be neutral ~N(0,1000) and Uniform(0,1000) for SD parameters.
497   Neutral and skeptical priors will be used for interaction terms. Point estimates of treatment effect and 95%
498   credible intervals will be reported along with probability of treatment benefit/harm. We will conduct similar
499   subgroup analyses to examine differences in treatment effect according to gender, race/ethnicity, although
500   such differences are not expected (required for NIH Phase III RCTs).
501
502    Construction and Testing of a Prediction Tool. We will use the final subgroup Bayesian models to develop and
503   test a potential web-based tool. The models will include only covariates defining subgroups that have a
504   differential treatment effect (i.e., large probability of interaction between covariate and treatment group). This
505   tool will be designed to help clinicians and parents assess the risks and benefits of early and late repair with
506   regards to SAEs, hospital days, and Bayley scores < 70 (impairment) based on the patient characteristics
507   noted above. Validity will be rigorously assessed in the study population using methods similar to that
508   previously used by Tyson et al (to help assess the effects of intensive and comfort care on various outcomes
509   for extremely premature infants).83 We will use posterior predictive checks (i.e., simulated data sets from the
510   final model are compared to observed data for major discrepancies) and ROC curves (where appropriate) to
      HIP Trial Protocol
                                                                                                                          14
511   assess the accuracy of the prediction tool relative to that of assuming the overall better timing of IH repair is
512   better for all patients.100 To guide decision-making, the web-based tool will take patients’ characteristics and
513   provide estimated probabilities of SAEs, number of hospital days, and probability of impairment with early and
514   late repair. To augment parental comprehension, probabilities will be represented graphically as well as with
515   percentages.101 These analyses are considered novel and secondary to the main purpose of the HIP trial. To
516   the extent that significant differences are found related to specific patient subgroups, these analyses may help
517   implement evidence-based surgical decision making.

519    Interim Analyses and Early Stopping Rules: The primary statistician (Dr. Pedroza) will conduct a safety and
520   efficacy analysis in December 2019, when we expect to have 10-month outcome data for approximately 200
521   infants. Bayesian methods will be used to calculate the probability of harm using the pre specified outcomes of
522   death and prolonged hospital stay >30 days post surgery. We will also conduct an efficacy Bayesian interim
523   analysis to calculate the probability of benefit on the primary outcome, proportion of infants with an SAE, and
524   secondary outcome of total hospital length of stay. We will use neutral priors that a priori assume a 50-50
525   likelihood of either repair management being better. In contrast to frequentist analyses, Bayesian analyses
526   provide direct probabilities of benefit and may be more interpretable by clinicians. In deciding whether to stop
527   or continue the trial, we define the following stopping guidelines:
528        a. Stopping for safety: probability of increased harm >90% for either arm (for either death or prolonged
529           hospital stay)
530        b. Stopping for efficacy: probability of decreased SAEs >95% for either arm

595   Interpretation of Results in Formulating Conclusions. Large RCTs are needed to provide the most rigorous and
596   unbiased safety assessments for major interventions, including IH repair as the most common major surgical
597   procedure for premature infants. The proposed trial will be subsidized by the participating institutions, led by
598   investigators exceptionally experienced in successfully designing and conducting difficult RCTs at relatively low
599   cost, and is designed to be the largest RCT feasible under an R01 mechanism. We expect adequate power to
600   assess differences between groups in SAEs and high power to assess differences in hospital days. While our
601   sample size estimates reflect our honest expectations based on multiple data sources, we acknowledge that
602   enrollment rates might not met expectations. If enrollment is less than expected, we could stop the study early,
603   resort to observational studies highly susceptible to bias, or choose to do no study at all, a reason that the
604   hazards of widely use therapies may go unsuspected for years.8 As Schulz and Grimes note, unbiased trials
605   with imprecise results trump no results at all.103 We propose instead to use frequentist and Bayesian analyses
606   as described above and base our conclusions about safety on the totality of evidence from the study. A
607   significant overall benefit for a major secondary outcome (e.g. number of hospital days during the study period)
608   can be a legitimate basis for recommending a therapy providing the primary outcome (e.g. proportion of infants
609   with > 1 SAE) and any other important outcome is in the same direction. For this reason, we would recommend
610   that late IH repair be routinely performed if it was associated with a significant reduction in total hospital days
611   as long as the SAEs and Bayley III scores did not favor the early group, particularly if there were a >80%
612   posterior probability that SAEs were less common and Bayley III scores higher with late than early IH repair.
