28   1. Overview
29   The purpose of this Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) is to provide all relevant information of the
30   planned analyses for the HIP Trial as specified in the study protocol and to clarify any potential
31   points of confusion in the original protocol. The planned analyses identified in this SAP will be
32   included in future manuscripts as deemed necessary by the HIP subcommittee. It is not the
33   intent of this SAP to identify all possible analyses that may be completed using the HIP
34   database. The purpose of this SAP is only to describe the analyses required to address the
35   objectives/specific aims/hypotheses explicitly stated in the protocol. Additional exploratory
36   analyses not identified in the protocol or not included in this SAP may be performed by the HIP
37   subcommittee.

38   2. Study Background and Rationale
39   The HIP Trial is a multicenter randomized clinical trial (RCT) conducted to determine whether
40   early inguinal hernia (IH) repair (prior to neonatal intensive care unit [NICU] discharge) or late
41   inguinal hernia repair (>55 weeks post menstrual age and after NICU discharge) is the safer
42   surgical approach for premature infants who are diagnosed with an inguinal hernia while in the
43   NICU. Because the timing (and duration) of general anesthesia is believed to be an important
44   determinant of neurodevelopmental outcomes beyond infancy, the HIP trial will also compare
45   neurodevelopmental outcomes, as assessed by the Bayley Scales of Infant Development, 3rd
46   edition (BSIDIII), at 2 years corrected age, for infants in the early versus late IH repair groups.

47   3. Study Hypotheses and Objectives
48   Primary objective
49   To compare the overall safety of early and late IH repair by comparing the proportion of infants
50   with >1 serious adverse event, measured from enrollment through 10 months later.
51   Secondary Objectives
52      1. To compare the number of hospital days from enrollment through 10 months beyond
53         enrollment between early and late IH repair, as a measure of the impact of adverse
54         events and resource utilization between the two groups.
55
56      2. To compare the safety of early and late IH repair with respect to neurodevelopmental
57         outcome at 22-26 months corrected age by comparing the cognitive composite BSIDIII
58         scores between groups.
59
60      3. To assess differences between early and late IH repair groups with respect to secondary
61         safety outcomes.
62
63      4. Using Bayesian and frequentist analyses, evaluate specific hypotheses about baseline
64         patient characteristics that may influence the relative safety of early and late IH repair.
65
66   Primary Hypothesis
67   Late IH repair (> 55 weeks post-menstrual age [PMA], which is expected to be ~5 months after
68   NICU discharge in most cases) in premature neonates with an IH will result in a 10% absolute
69   reduction in the proportion of infants with > 1 serious adverse event (SAE) compared to early IH
70    repair (prior to NICU discharge, ~ 38 weeks PMA). We estimate 30% of infants in the early
71    repair group will have at least one SAE compared to 20% in the late IH repair group.
72    Secondary Hypotheses
73    1. Late IH repair will be associated with a 3-day reduction in the median total number of
74       hospital days during the study observation period (i.e., enrollment until 10 months later)
75       compared to early IH repair (8 total hospital days for early IH repair versus 5 for late repair).
76
77       **Clarification of inconsistency in final trial protocol: in the original trial protocol, the expected
78       median total number of hospital days was listed as 18 for early IH repair and 15 for the late
79       repair group (page 3), which is incorrect. The correct values were presented on page 12 of
80       the final protocol and are as written here.
81
82    2. Late IH repair will be associated with a 0.4 SD increase in mean scores on the BSIDIII
83       Cognitive Composite score (6 points absolute difference) compared to early IH repair.
84
85    3. Despite an overall treatment effect favoring late IH repair with respect to SAEs, the actual
86       treatment effect will vary for pre-defined and specified patient subgroups defined in the trial
87       protocol (“treatment effect modifiers” section, page 13).

89    4. Trial Methods
90    4.1 Trial Design
91    The HIP trial is a randomized, multicenter parallel-group trial. Infants are randomized 1:1 to
92    early or late IH repair.
93    4.2 Treatment Groups
94    a. Early IH Repair: IH repair is performed after randomization, when the neonatology team
95    believes the infant is optimized from a medical perspective, and prior to NICU discharge; ~38
96    weeks PMA, based on pilot trial and experience in practice.
 97   b. Late IH Repair: IH repair will be performed when infant is > 55 weeks post menstrual age
 98   and after NICU discharge. This is estimated to typically be approximately 5 months after NICU
 99   discharge. This PMA was selected as the goal time for late repair because most children’s
100   hospitals consider allowing the operation to be done as an outpatient at this time and this
101   reflects current surgical care. The decision regarding admission will be determined by the
102   providers for each infant according to site guidelines.
103   4.3 Randomization
104   Randomization is stratified by site and GA stratum (<28 weeks and >28 weeks estimated GA)
105   using permuted variable block size. Randomization occurred 24 hours a day, 7 days a week
106   through a REDCap electronic data capture system. In cases of multiple gestations qualifying for
107   inclusion the first twin was randomized in the usual manner and the sibling was assigned to the
108   same treatment group.
109   4.4 Sample Size
110   To test the primary hypothesis that overall SAE rates will be 30% in the early repair group
111   compared to 20% in the late repair group 293 infants per group (N = 586 total) are required to
112   have 80% power (two-sided alpha of 0.05) to detect a 10% absolute difference. With a 95%
113   follow-up rate, we plan on randomizing 615 infants. For the secondary outcomes, we will have
114   98% power to detect a median difference of 3 hospital days (assuming median=8, mean=18 for
115   early group and median=5, mean=13 for the late group and a log normal distribution). For
116   BSIDIII scores, we will be able to detect a 0.4 SD difference (6 points on the BSIDIII) assuming
117   200 infants are assessed at 22-26 months.
118   4.5 Interim Analysis
119   One formal statistical interim analysis was planned on the primary endpoint when 10-month
120   data was available for 200 randomized infants. Further details of this analysis and stopping
121   guidelines are provided in the study protocol (Page 14).
122   4.6 Timing of final analysis
123   Final analysis of the early vs late IH repair comparison was planned to take place when 10-
124   month outcome data was available for 586 randomized infants. However, in April 2021, the HIP
125   trial stopped enrollment after an interim analysis found a 97% probability of decreased rate of
126   SAEs in the late group. This probability crossed the pre-specified efficacy stopping threshold of
127   95%. Consistent with our overall approach, the interim analyses were primarily Bayesian.
128   4.7 Timing of outcome assessments
129   Primary outcome is assessed at 10 months post-randomization. Neurodevelopmental outcomes
130   are collected at 22-26 months corrected age. More details are presented in the study protocol.

131   5. Statistical Principles
132   5.1 Analysis framework and reporting procedures
133   Complementary frequentist and Bayesian analyses will be conducted for the primary outcome
134   (proportion of children with ≥ 1 SAE) and important secondary outcomes of death, total hospital
135   days, and BSIDIII scores. Conduct and reporting of both frequentist and Bayesian results has
136   been previously recommended to provide a thorough presentation of the evidence of an
137   intervention effect.1 While frequentist analyses are typically reported for RCTs of medical
138   interventions, the results are often erroneously dichotomized into “significant” and “non-
139   significant” based only on the p-value. Importantly, frequentist statistics cannot provide an
140   important quantity, the direct probability of treatment benefit or harm from an intervention.
141   Bayesian statistics calculate this probability that can be directly used by clinicians and parents
142   when deciding on treatment options and for these reasons we consider our Bayesian analyses
143   to be primary and frequentist analyses to be secondary. This important emphasis has been
144   made prior to the conduct of any final analyses. Our investigator group has been involved in
145   several RCTs using this approach and have been influenced by other recent publications.2-5
146   We will report estimates of treatment effect (relative risk and risk difference, group mean
147   differences) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for all frequentist analyses. For Bayesian
148   analyses, we will report posterior medians, 95% credible intervals (CrI), and probabilities of any
149   intervention benefit/harm and probability of a clinically important intervention effect.
150   5.2 Protocol Deviations
151   Protocol deviations were collected using a specified set of descriptions. Possible values for
152   protocol deviations were:
153          Study intervention not completed at specified time (either early or late IH repair)
154          Wrong treatment intervention applied
155          Infant ineligible
156          No consent
157          Wrong stratification variables used at randomization
158          Other violation
159
160   Protocol deviations will be descriptively summarized in terms of infants per arm with any
161   deviations and in terms of number of deviations per arm.
162   5.3 Analyses Populations
163   We will conduct analyses in two study populations, a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) and per-
164   protocol (PP) populations. The mITT analysis dataset will include all randomized infants with
165   ascertained primary outcomes. The PP analysis dataset will include all infants who received
166   their randomized intervention (early or late IH repair) and have a determined primary outcome.

167   6. Trial Population
168   6.1 Screening population
169   All infants born at < 37 weeks gestation with a diagnosis of IH at a participating site are
170   screened for inclusion in the trial. Total number of eligible patients, number of eligible not
171   enrolled, and reason for non-recruitment will be reported.
172   Eligibility
173   Inclusion Criteria: 1) GA < 37 weeks, 0 days; 2) In a NICU / other nursery, at participating sites;
174   3) Diagnosed with an IH per the pediatric surgeon; and 4) Parental consent and providers willing
175   to randomize the infant.
176   Exclusion Criteria:
177   1. Associated factor that impacts timing of repair (e.g. clinical factors that might preclude early
178   IH repair)
179   2) Infant is undergoing another operative procedure and IH repair is planned as a secondary
180   procedure such that timing of IH repair is impacted (e.g., fundoplication or gastrostomy tube is
181   planned, and IH repair is considered a secondary procedure)
182   3) Known major congenital anomaly or chromosomal abnormality
183   4) Family unable to return for follow up and late IH repair or likely unable to monitor IH as
184   outpatient (e.g., maternal incarceration or moving away from the area).
185   A CONSORT flow diagram will be used to summarize the number of patients who were: eligible
186   and randomized; eligible but not randomized (reasons will be presented); received the
187   randomized intervention; did not receive the randomized intervention; lost to follow-up;
188   discontinued the intervention; randomized and included in the primary analysis; randomized and
189   excluded from the primary analysis (reasons will be presented).
190   6.2 Withdrawal / Follow-up
191         Infants can be lost-to-follow-up between NICU discharge and the time of their scheduled
192          repair (for late IH repair) or their 10-month follow-up phone call. Infants in centers with
193          neurodevelopmental assessments can also be lost between 10-month and 22-26 month
194          follow-up assessment.
195         Infants who withdraw from the study will not have any data collected beyond the
196          timepoint of withdrawal. Data collected prior to withdrawal will be available for
197          summarization and analysis.
198         Numbers of infants who withdraw or are lost to follow-up will be presented in the
199          CONSORT diagram.

259   7.3 Analysis Methods
260   All outcomes will be summarized by treatment group. Categorical outcomes will be summarized
261   by numbers and percentages. Count outcomes will be summarized by median, IQR, mean, and
262   SD. Continuous normal outcomes will be summarized by mean and SD.
263   Multilevel regression models will be used to analyze all outcomes. For each outcome, the same
264   model will be used for the frequentist and Bayesian analysis. Binary outcomes will be analyzed
265   with a logistic model. Ordinal outcomes will be analyzed with a proportional-odds logistic model.
266   Count outcomes will be analyzed with a negative binomial model. Continuous outcomes will be
267   analyzed with a normal regression model. All models will include a random intercept for site and
268   will include GA group (< 28 weeks and ≥ 28 weeks estimated GA) as a covariate.
269   We will report relative risks (RR) and risk differences (RD) for binary outcomes (calculated from
270   logistic model), RRs for count outcomes, odds ratios (OR) for ordinal outcomes, and mean
271   group differences for continuous outcomes. We will report 95% CIs (95% CrIs for Bayesian
272   results) for all outcomes. For probabilities of benefit/harm, we will report probabilities of any
273   effect and probabilities of at least 3% and 5% reduction in the risk for SAE. Although we
274   hypothesized a 10% reduction in the proportion of infants in the late IH repair group, most
275   stakeholders would likely agree that a smaller reduction (3 or 5%) is above the minimal clinically
276   important difference.
277   All analyses will be conducted in R. Bayesian models will be fit using Markov chain Monte Carlo
278   (MCMC) methods implemented in Stan via R packages ‘rstanarm’ and ‘brms’. Each model will
279   be fit using three MCMC chains. Each chain will involve at least 5,000 burn-in samples and at
280   least 50,000 additional iterations.
281
282   7.3.1 Bayesian prior specifications

283   Bayesian analyses will use neutral priors for all outcomes, since there has never been a
284   previous RCT comparing these two treatment options. For categorical outcomes, we will
285   assume a prior centered at OR of 1.0 with 95% CrI of 0.2-4 for the intervention effect. This will
286   take the form of a Normal distribution with mean of 0 and SD of 0.7 in the log OR scale. For
287   count outcomes, the prior for the intervention effect will be centered at RR of 1.0 with 95% CrI of
288   0.33-3.3. For the intercept we will use a vague Normal(0, SD=10) distribution and for GA group
289   a weakly informative Normal(0, SD=1) prior. For the standard deviation of the random intercept,
290   we will use a half-Normal(0, SD=1) prior distribution.
291   For continuous outcomes, we specify priors for standardized variables. For the intervention
292   effect we will use a Normal(0, SD=2) prior. For the intercept and GA group we will use
293   Normal(0, SD=10) and Normal(0, SD=3) priors, respectively. For the standard deviation of the
294   random intercept, we will use a half-Normal(0, SD=2) prior distribution.
295   7.3.2 Assumption Checks

296   Assumptions of frequentist models will be evaluated using residual plots.
297   For Bayesian models, convergence of MCMC chains will involve visual comparisons of the trace
298   plots from the 3 chains generated for each model. Quantitative checks such as the Geweke
299   and Gelman/Rubin tests will be completed to ensure convergence to the posterior distribution.
300   For Bayesian models, posterior predictive checks will be used to determine if the resulting
301   model mimics the data sufficiently well for the inferences that will be made from this trial. The
302   posterior predictive check will involve simulating 1,000 trials from the posterior predictive
303   distributions of the model parameters and summarizing the average probability/number of
304   counts/mean of the outcome by:

305         Treatment
306         GA group
307   When the above 1000 simulated trial averages are plotted by the above categories and the
308   observed proportions are overlayed on the plots, then the observed proportions should lie within
309   the cloud of points generated by the simulations and not appear to be outliers.
310   7.3.3 Sensitivity Analysis

311   In addition to adjusting for GA group and center as a random effect, sensitivity analyses will be
312   conducted if important baseline covariates are imbalanced across the two intervention groups.
313   This analysis will only be completed for the primary outcome and will be based on the mITT
314   sample.
315   For the primary outcome, in addition to the mITT sample-based analysis, a PP analysis will be
316   done using the PP sample.
317   7.3.4 Subgroup Analyses

318   The primary outcome will be reanalyzed for subgroup exploratory analyses. These analyses will
319   be completed using the mITT sample. To investigate treatment effect modifiers for the primary
320   and secondary outcomes (total hospital days and BSIDIII scores), we will use Bayesian
321   hierarchical models with terms of interaction between treatment group (early, late) and the
322   prespecified potential moderators: GA (<28; >28 weeks) bronchopulmonary dysplasia (yes/no),
323   maternal education <12 years (yes/no), prenatal care (yes/no), IH difficult to reduce (yes/no).
324   The conservative Bayesian approach of Dixon and Simon6 allows us to specify a priori how
325   likely (or unlikely) it is for subgroup differences to be present and to shrink the subgroup
326   estimates to the overall mean treatment effect.
327   Prior distributions for main effects will be the same as for the primary analysis. A Normal(0,
328   SD=0.6) will be used for interaction terms. Point estimates of treatment effect and 95% credible
329   intervals for each subgroup will be reported along with probability of treatment benefit/harm. We
330   will conduct similar subgroup analyses to examine differences in treatment effect according to
331   gender, race/ethnicity, although such differences are not expected (required for NIH Phase III
332   RCTs).
333   Two additional exploratory subgroup analyses will be performed (not stated in trial protocol): the
334   impact of laparoscopic versus open IH repair on primary and secondary outcomes has become
335   more important given the increasing use of laparoscopic approach to these operations. The
336   distance of the primary residence of the family to the treating facility will also be evaluated to
337   investigate whether longer distance from the facility might impact the treatment effect by
338   favoring early repair.
339   7.3.5 Analyses of association between gestational age and risk of SAE

340   In addition to the planned subgroup analyses by GA group, we will also conduct analyses to
341   investigate the effect of GA on the rate of SAE. We will treat GA as a continuous variable in the
342   model and using splines or polynomials we will evaluate whether a linear or non-linear
343   relationship provides the best fit for the data. We will use splines or polynomial to model the
344   association. All other model details will be as described for the primary analysis.
345   We will then evaluate an interaction between the final GA term (linear or non-linear) and
346   intervention effect for the primary outcome using the same methods described in the Subgroup
347   Analyses section.
348   7.3.6 Ad-hoc Analyses

349   Other ad hoc analyses may be done in addition to those specified here at the primary and
350   secondary papers are developed. These will be clearly labeled in any publication as ad hoc
351   analyses motivated by our findings as we completed the planned data analyses.
352   7.4 Missing Data
353   No imputation for missing data will be done.
