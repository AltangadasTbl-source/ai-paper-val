A randomised trial of krill oil for osteoarthritis of the knee

  Background: Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a significant and increasing cause of pain and
  disability in the Australian population. So far, interventions aimed at structural modification
  have been disappointing. OA affects the whole joint, and inflammation of the joint lining
  (synovitis and joint fluid) is now recognised as a key part of osteoarthritis.
  Animal studies suggest that krill oil is anti–inflammatory. Pilot data in humans shows that krill
  oil reduces knee pain and systemic inflammation after 30 days, suggesting that an approach
  targeted at specific mechanisms of disease may be effective for OA. However, this study was
  of short duration, did not collect any data on structural factors, and included patients with a
  variety of disorders. Longer, larger follow up studies are required.
  Aim: To compare, using a randomised, placebo–controlled double–blind design over six
  months, the efficacy of krill oil vs identical placebo to treat knee osteoarthritis (OA) (both pain
  and structure) in 260 patients with clinical knee OA, significant knee pain and effusion on
  imaging.
  Primary hypothesis:
  Krill oil decreases pain (assessed by 100mm VAS) by 10mm more than identical placebo over
  24 weeks in patients with clinical knee OA, significant knee pain and effusions.
  Secondary hypotheses:
  Krill oil decreases effusion size by 20% over 24 weeks in patients with symptomatic
  osteoarthritis of the knee and knee effusion.
  Krill oil improves knee pain over 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 weeks in patients with clinical knee OA
  and effusion.
  Significance and Innovation: If krill oil can both improve symptoms and decrease effusion in
  OA it has the potential to slow progression to joint replacement. The proposed study represents
  an innovative approach to this and lends itself to easy implementation as krill oil is already
  available over–the–counter in Australian pharmacies.



Background
Osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the leading causes of disability globally. In Australia, OA affects over
1.9 million people (8.6% of the population – 2012 estimates), with health expenditure of $3.75
billion in 20121. The majority of the cost is hip and knee replacements1, 2, with over 40,000 of these
procedures performed in 2006–07, each costing an average of $15,000–$30,0002.
Despite its large disease burden, there are currently no approved disease–modifying drugs available
which modify structural progression of OA. Current treatments focus on alleviating pain, which is
important because patients rank pain control as their highest treatment priority3. Despite this, pain
control remains poor in 50% of patients4. Therefore, there is a significant gap between the level of
pain relief patients want, and what they actually get. Lack of treatment efficacy may be partly due
to treating everyone with OA as if they have the same pathological process.
Modern approaches to OA involve targeting specific disease pathways
OA is now considered to be a collection of different disease pathways that result in a common
outcome5, 6, rather than one disease with a common pathway. There are many sources of pain, and
many factors associated with loss of cartilage (Table 1)7, and thereby progression of disease. Our
approach is that targeting specific sources of pain and structural damage is likely to improve
outcomes in individual patients. We have successfully used this approach, targeting bone marrow
lesions, where we produced the first evidence of a treatment that had a larger effect on pain and

Inflammation is an important factor in knee pain and knee OA pathogenesis
1. Systemic inflammation is associated with knee pain and predicts progression in OA
OA is generally thought of as a ‘non-inflammatory’ type of arthritis; however, localised low-grade
inflammation is now known to be an important factor in OA pathogenesis. Pro–inflammatory
cytokines, including interleukin (IL)–1β, tumour necrosis factor–α (TNF– α) , and IL–6 are
produced by synovium and chondrocytes, and contribute to the progression of cartilage
degradation9. The inflammatory pattern is observed both early10 and late9 in the disease process.
This local inflammation can be observed systemically using assays for high sensitive C reactive
protein (hsCRP), as hsCRP levels are positively correlated with the degree of synovial
inflammatory infiltration in OA11. A meta-analysis from our centre demonstrated that hsCRP levels
are modestly, but significantly, elevated in persons with OA compared to controls12. Importantly,
hsCRP levels are associated with both symptoms and disease markers, with higher hsCRP levels
associated with greater pain (in both this meta–analysis12, and longitudinal data from our centre12,
13
   ), decreased physical function12, decreased cartilage volume14 and disease progression10, 15.
Therefore, systemic inflammation predicts both pain and structural outcomes in OA.
2. Local inflammation (effusion–synovitis) is associated with pain and predicts progression of OA
Effusion (excess synovial fluid within the joint space) and /or synovitis (thickening of the
synovium), represent localised inflammation within the knee joint. This can be visualised using
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), a highly sensitive method of detecting and measuring knee
structural abnormalities. Effusion–synovitis is seen on T2 MRI as increased signal intensity in the
joint spaces (see Figure 1).

Like hsCRP, inflamed synovia and/or effusions are important because they predict pain16-19,
especially non-weight bearing knee pain19. Importantly, such associations are localised20, with
effusions having independent compartment–specific associations with knee pain19. Our pilot data
from the TASOAC study shows that this is common, affecting 67% of randomly selected
participants aged ≥50 in Hobart21, including 22% with large effusions (effusion–synovitis score ≥3).
Our data demonstrates that having effusion–synovitis at baseline was more common amongst those
who went on to develop new knee pain, or whose pain worsened over 2.6 years [to be presented as
an oral presentation at Osteoarthritis Research Society International, Seattle, 2015]. Having
effusion–synovitis in three of the four anatomic regions in the knee was associated with presence of
non–weight bearing pain (Figure 2); and the proportion of study participants with increased knee
pain over time was larger in those with larger areas of size of effusion–synovitis on MRI scans
(Figure 3). Links between synovial inflammation and knee structural changes are a vicious cycle,
with synovial inflammation causing knee structural changes22, and knee structural changes causing
synovial inflammation22, 23.

Therefore, effusion–synovitis is associated with increased knee pain over time in a dose–response,
compartment–specific, manner, and represents localised inflammation. Stopping the cascade of
inflammation is likely to slow down deleterious changes in knee structure.
Effusions / synovitis predict cartilage loss and joint replacement; therefore, treating these may
slow disease progression
Data from our centre (Menzies, Hobart – TASOAC study) recently published in the top journal of
the field also demonstrates that effusion–synovitis in the suprapatellar pouch predicts cartilage
defects, bone marrow lesions (BMLs) and increased cartilage loss over 2.6 years 21 (See Figure 4).
This is consistent with data from arthoscopy22, and with other longitudinal data that demonstrates
that effusion predicts joint replacement24. Thus, reversing inflammation has the potential to
improve outcomes globally in knee OA.
High levels of inflammation in OA can be treated, but existing options have side effects
A recent RCT in knee OA demonstrated that elevated levels of systemic inflammation can be
modified25, with oral low-dose prednisolone reducing hsCRP in people with elevated hsCRP and
clinical synovitis by as early as 6 weeks. However, prednisolone is associated with a well–
characterised suite of adverse events in the short and long term, and therefore such treatments are
not feasible for all people with OA and systemic inflammation. Inflammation can also be reduced
using treatment with biological therapy e.g. adalimumab, but these therapies are expensive, and
have too many side effects for widespread use. Cyclooxygenase inhibitors e.g. Celebrex are
labelled non–steroidal anti–inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), yet do not change CRP levels26, casting

Fish oil reduces systemic inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis but not osteoarthritis
Fish oil has been successfully used for treating rheumatoid arthritis (RA) since the early 1990’s.
The omega-3 fatty acids, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) can
suppress synthesis of the omega–6 pro–inflammatory eicosanoids, prostaglandin E; and leukotriene
B427. Overall, RCT’s in RA demonstrate that fish oil reduces pain, morning stiffness, number of
painful and/or tender joints and NSAID consumption28; and is an effective add–on therapy to
standard RA therapies, reducing risk of treatment failure and increasing rate of remission29.
Therefore, fish oil is an effective treatment for RA.
However, data on efficacy of fish oil in OA is limited. Our Network conducted a 24–month RCT of
high dose fish oil vs low dose fish oil / canola oil in participants with knee OA (CIC Hill, Annals of
the Rheumatic Diseases, under revision). Intensity of knee OA symptoms reduced more in the low
dose group at 24 months; suggesting that fish oil is ineffective for OA, but canola oil may be
effective.
Krill oil is a better alternative for osteoarthritis than fish oil
Krill oil, which is extracted from Antarctic Krill (Ephausia Superba, a zooplankton crustacean) has
emerged on the market in recent years. Like fish oil, it is high in EPA and DHA30, although the
chemical structures of the fatty acids differ (phospholipids, rather than triacylglycerol or fatty acid
ethyl esters); but unlike fish oil, it also naturally contains antioxidants (predominantly
astaxanthin30). Additionally, bioavailability of krill oil is better than fish oil, as comparable
amounts of EPA and DHA are obtained from lower doses of krill oil compared to fish oil31.
In animal studies, krill oil supplementation had larger effects on most clinical outcomes than fish
oil32 while krill oil reduced the severity of inflammatory arthritis in mice by 50% compared to
controls32. In mice transgenic for human TNF–α, both fish and krill oil improved plasma
cholesterol levels, but only krill oil had additional beneficial effects on markers of fatty acid
oxidation33. Therefore bioavailability data and animal studies suggest that krill oil has anti-
inflammatory and anti-oxidative effects, and may be a better treatment in vivo than fish oil.
Pilot data in humans: Deutsch et al34
Only one RCT has assessed the effect of krill oil on inflammation and arthritis symptoms34. This
RCT included 90 participants aged 30–75 with cardiovascular disease, RA or OA, and raised CRP
levels. Participants treated with 300mg krill oil over 30 days had less pain on the Western Ontario
and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) pain scale (change scores -38.35±21.06
[krill oil] vs -0.6±15.89 [placebo], p=0.01), and reductions in CRP levels over 30 days (-30.9% ±1.0
vs +25.1%±1.05, p=0.008), compared to placebo. Similar effects were observed with stiffness and
functional impairment scores. These results demonstrate that krill oil begins to work by 4 weeks,

and potentially as early as 14 days. This suggests that krill oil reduces knee pain and systemic
inflammation, within 2-4 weeks. No adverse events were reported.
The Deutch et al.34 trial is an important proof of principle study. It demonstrates improvements in
pain, stiffness, functional impairment, and systemic inflammation. However, there were
limitations.
1. The follow up period was short (30 days).
2. No imaging data was collected; therefore, effect of krill oil on structural changes is unknown.
3. The study population was a mixture of people with disorders that increase systemic
   inflammation (cardiovascular disease, RA and OA), and no subgroup results were described.
   There were numerous exclusion criteria that were not justified by contra–indications to krill oil,
   including any concurrent medical condition or concomitant treatments, limiting the
   generalisability of the study findings.
4. Adverse events were poorly documented.
5. The study was not independent, being funded by a pharmaceutical company.
6. The dose of krill oil used in this trial (300mg/day) is much lower than that currently available
   over-the-counter in Australia (and hence used by consumers), as well as being much lower than
   the dose recommended by experts as being truly anti-inflammatory (2-3 g/day) (personal
   communication, Trygve Bergeland, Aker Biomarine).

Therefore, we plan to conduct a six month RCT of krill oil 2g/day (vs placebo) to assess the effect
on knee pain and size of knee effusions in people with at least moderate knee pain and knee
effusions on MRI. This will target an inflammatory subtype of OA with a treatment that has been
shown to have anti–inflammatory effects.
This is the first trial to measure change in effusion–synovitis; therefore this approach is cutting
edge. Measurement of these features is now possible due to advances in MRI technology and the
expertise of our Network.
Research Plan
Study aim
To compare, using a randomised, placebo–controlled double–blind design over six months, the
effect of 2g krill oil daily compared to identical placebo, on knee pain (primary outcome) and also
MRI–detected knee effusion in 260 participants with clinical knee OA, significant knee pain and
effusions on imaging.
Study design
Multi–centre randomised, placebo–controlled double blind clinical trial.
Primary outcome
Change in knee pain (assessed by visual analog score (VAS)) over 24 weeks.
Secondary outcomes
1. Change in maximal area and volume of knee effusion–synovitis, 24 weeks.
2. Change in ordinal measures of knee effusion, 24 weeks.
3. Change in BML maximal area, 24 weeks.
4. Change in ultrasound parameters, 24 weeks
5. Change in knee pain (VAS score), other time points (4, 8, 12, 16, 20 weeks).
6. Change in knee pain (total WOMAC pain, weight bearing and non–weight bearing pain), all
    time points.
7. Change in hand and back pain (VAS), all time points.
8. Change in knee function, all time points.
9. OMERACT–OARSI responder criteria35, all time points.
10. Change in hsCRP, 12 and 24 weeks.
11. Change in blood lipids, 12 and 24 weeks.

Study participants
We will recruit 260 participants from five centres (Hobart, Melbourne, Sydney, Adelaide and Perth)
with significant knee pain and presence of MRI defined effusion–synovitis. We will use a proven
strategy, including collaboration with general practitioners, specialist rheumatologists, orthopaedic
surgeons, and advertising through TV, newspaper and social media. This is the approach we have
taken to successfully recruit participants for large community–based trials across a range of
musculoskeletal conditions.
Inclusion criteria
1. Aged over 40 years old.
2. Men and women with significant knee pain on most days (defined as a VAS >40mm).
3. Knee effusion–synovitis on MRI: In TASOAC the proportion of participants who had effusion–
   synovitis on MRI was high (67%), suggesting that localised knee inflammation is common, and
   that MRI is an appropriate screening tool.
4. Meet American College of Rheumatology (ACR) clinical criteria for knee OA confirmed by a
   rheumatologist.
Exclusion criteria
1. Inability to have an MRI (claustrophobia, metal in eyes).
2. Severe knee OA (joint space narrowing (JSN) on X-ray of Grade 3 using the Osteoarthritis
    Research Society International (OARSI) atlas36).
3. Use of anticoagulants, high dose aspirin or non-steroidal anti–inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), as
    krill oil is contra–indicated in such people.
4. Unwillingness to stop taking krill oil and fish oil medications during the trial.
5. High dietary fish intake (≥1 fish / seafood meal each week)
6. Other forms of inflammatory arthritis (especially rheumatoid arthritis and gout).
7. Seafood allergy.
8. Significant knee injury within the last 6 months.
9. Arthroscopy or open surgery in the index knee in the last 12 months, or planned.
10. Injections of corticosteroids (last 3 months) or hyaluronic acid (last 6 months) in the index knee.
11. Pregnancy or breastfeeding.
12. Use of any investigational drug(s) and/or devices within 30 days prior to randomisation
13. Presence of any serious medical illness that may preclude 24 week follow up.
14. Inability to provide informed consent.
Treatment
Eligible participants will receive two capsules of krill oil or inert identical placebo daily for 24
weeks. Allocation of participants to active or placebo will be based on computer generated random
numbers, by site, using adaptive allocation. Allocation concealment will be ensured by using
identical capsules, created for us by a compounding pharmacy. Use of a centralised, secure
allocation procedure will ensure allocation data cannot be accessed or influenced by any person.

Outcome measures
During the 24 week study, there will be 4 study visits (screening, baseline / week 0, weeks 12, 24) –
see Table 2. Additional questionnaires will occur at weeks 4, 8, 16 and 20. MRI’s will occur at
screening, and week 24. Knee X–ray will occur only at screening, for classification purposes.
1. Primary outcome: Pain
Pain and function will be assessed at each time point using a 100mm visual analogue scale (VAS)
and asked “On this line, how would you rate your pain today”? We will also assess pain and
function using WOMAC37.
2. MRI measure of change in effusion size
An MRI scan of the “study” knee will be performed (screening, week 24). Knees will be imaged in
the sagittal plane on a 1.5T whole-body magnetic resonance unit (GE-Signa) using a dedicated 8–
channel knee coil, using a proton density–weighted fat saturation 2-dimensional (2-D) fast spin
echo sequence (T2). Effusions are defined as the presence of intra–articular fluid–equivalent signal
on T2 MRI sequences. These will be measured using software cursors.
Effusion size in each of 4 regions of interest (ROI; suprapatellar pouch, central portion, posterior
femoral recess, subpopliteal recess) will be summed together for each slice. The greatest score for
the sum of the four ROI (mm2) will be used as maximal effusion. Intra–observer repeatability for
this measurement method is excellent, with an ICC of 0.81. This is superior to an ordinal measure
of effusion–synovitis size, with kappa 0.63–0.75 in different sub–regions [in-house data].
Effusion volume will be measured in a similar way but with volumes for each slice summed over
the whole joint. Change in effusion–synovitis volume and area is calculated by subtracting baseline
values from follow–up maximal values.
Differentiating effusions from synovitis on MR images requires administration of a contrast agent
(gadolinium), which may cause nephrotoxicity. While this is rare, we do not consider the benefits
of such differentiation between synovitis and effusion justifies the risk to our study participants.
Other explanatory measures
3. Radiographic OA
A standing anteroposterior semiflexed radiograph of the ‘study knee’ will be performed at
screening. X-rays will be scored for joint space narrowing and osteophytes on a four point scale (0–
3) using the OARSI atlas36. In our hands this method has very high reproducibility with an ICC of
0.98 for joint space narrowing and 0.99 for osteophytes38.
4. Bone marrow lesions present on MRI
BMLs will be assessed on the MRI scans, and defined as an ill–defined hyperintensity in the
subchondral bone. One trained observer will score lesions on all MRIs at each time point; they will
be blinded to treatment allocation and clinical data. The maximum size of the lesion will be
measured in mm2 using software cursors applied to the greatest area of each lesion, as previously
described8, 39. Intraobserver repeatability for this measurement method is excellent, with ICC of
0.9739.
5. Ultrasound parameters
Presence and depth of effusion, synovitis score, presence of synovial hypertrophy and synovial
power Doppler signal at the suprapatellar pouch (SPP), lateral and medial para patellar pouches will
be assessed. There is very limited data on ultrasound parameters vs MRI in knee OA, therefore this
data will add to the field.
6. Blood markers: Inflammation, lipids, glucose
Markers of systemic inflammation, including hsCRP, IL–6 and TNF–α, cholesterol (HDL, LDL),
and fatty acids will be assessed using standard assays, from blood samples taken at screening, week
12 and week 24. Changes in hsCRP levels should be detectable within 7 days of krill oil

treatment34. Previous studies show that krill oil reduces lipid levels in people with
hyperlipidaemia40. As one study has suggested that krill oil may reduce insulin sensitivity41, we
will monitor fasting blood glucose levels at each blood sampling.
7. Lower limb muscle strength
Lower limb muscle strength is a key correlate of pain and will be measured by dynamometry in the
lower limbs (involving both legs simultaneously) at weeks 0, 12 and 24. This technique mainly
measures quadriceps and hip flexors; these are the most important muscle groups for knee OA
based on our studies and those of others; strength in other muscle groups eg hamstrings are not
associated with knee OA42. Repeatability estimates (Cronbach’s α) were 0.9143. Dynamometer–
assessed leg strength improves longitudinally when pain reduces (unpublished data, 4Jointz trial)44.
8. Anthropometry
These include height (stadiometer) and weight (electronic scales) measured at weeks 0, 12 and 24.
9. Concomitant medication
Use of pain medicines will be recorded by questionnaire at all visits, including over-the-counter
remedies, interventions, or supplements. Participants will be asked to keep medications as stable as
possible (including NSAIDs) and use paracetamol as rescue medication, which will be provided by
us. Use of fish and krill oil during the trial will not be permitted, and use must cease 4 weeks prior
to randomisation. Participants who commence anti–coagulant therapy will be withdrawn from the
trial.
10. Compliance estimates
Participants will be asked to bring unused softgels with them to their clinic visits at 12 and 24
weeks. These will be counted, enabling estimates of treatment compliance. This is the best
method45, and is industry standard for measuring compliance.
11. Joint replacement
We plan to collect data on joint replacement, including date of surgery, by asking about knee
surgery at each time point, and also by retaining contact with study participants once the study is
complete. We will also report time to joint replacement if there is sufficient data. This will be able
to be collected both from the patient themselves and from the national joint replacement registry
using linked data.
12. Cost data / quality of life
The Assessment of Quality of Life (AQoL) questionnaire can be used to calculate utility values.
Data on medication use, adverse events and time away from work will be used as key inputs for
health economic evaluations. Cost-effectiveness analysis on the primary outcome measure and
cost-utility analysis with the AQoL will be possible if the primary endpoints are met. Cost data and
service use will be able to be assessed from linked data, including PBS and MBS data.
13. Service use / medication use
We plan to assess use of services, medications and other aspects of health by linking to datasets
available for linkage. These include Medicare and Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) datasets,
the National Death Index, the National Joint Replacement Registry, and state-based hospital
emergency databases and hospital inpatient databases, and other health datasets.
14. Pressure pain threshold testing
Pressure pain threshold testing (PPT) assesses sensitivity to pain. There is increasing evidence that
some people with OA have abnormal sensation in the areas of their body that are painful, including
pressure-induced pain46. Presence of abnormal sensation of this type has implications for response
to pharmaceutical treatment, hence will be assessed where facilities permit (Melbourne, Perth).
PPT will be conducted using a pressure algometer (FDN200, Wagner Instruments, USA), applied
over 3 test sites: the affected joint (knee, assessed over the patella), an unaffected joint (ipsilateral
radioulnar joint), and over the thenar eminence47. Fourteen-day test-retest reliability for PPT was

14. Adverse events
Drug safety will be assessed by recording all important adverse events (AE’s), whether we think

Statistics
Linear regression will be used to compare changes in pain, maximal effusion size and function in
univariate and multivariable modelling (if groups are not well matched at baseline) between groups
using intention to treat analysis for all outcomes. Per protocol analyses will be completed for study
participants consuming ≥80% of capsules. Significance will be p < 0.05 (two tailed). Missing data
will be accounted for by using propensity weighting, as appropriate.
Power calculations
Based on in–house data from the 4Jointz trial44, assuming 10mm difference between krill oil and
placebo on the VAS pain scale (reduction in VAS pain scores in the placebo group by -15.5±25.5
mm over 12 weeks), 90% power, and 5% probability of type 1 error (alpha=0.05), we will need 234
participants. Adjusting for 10% loss to follow up, we need 260 participants (130 in each arm).
While studies suggest that a 15 mm difference between groups is clinically meaningful,49 for a
natural therapy which has a better safety profile than pharmaceutical products, lower benefits are
also likely to be worthwhile.
Based on data from TASOAC, this will give us 96% power to detect a difference in effusion size of
20% (mean 2.24cm2, SD of change 1.35). What is clinically significant has not been defined, but
we will investigate associations between change in effusion size and change in pain in this trial. We
expect to see a natural fluctuation in knee effusion, but this effect is likely to be similar in
intervention and treatment groups. Any differences in effusion size between treatment vs placebo
groups can then be explained by the treatment, if the randomisation has worked as planned.
If we use data from the pilot study34, assuming a 20% relative difference in WOMAC scores
between groups and 90% power, and 5% probability of type 1 error (alpha=0.05), we will need a
total of n=54 participants over 4 weeks. However, we believe these figures are overly optimistic, as
participants in this trial were required to cease NSAIDs, and the trial had a number of potential
biases. We propose to conduct an add–on trial to usual practice rather than treatment cessation, as
this reflects the real world more accurately.

Outcomes and significance
OA is a major public health problem that is poorly managed, leaving patients in pain while the
disease progresses. Krill oil is cheap and safe, and already widely available in Australia. It appears
to successfully treat localised inflammation; therefore, it has excellent prospects for treating an
inflammatory subtype of knee OA. If this trial is positive, there will be compelling evidence that
krill oil will improve pain and function, change disease trajectory and slow disease progression, in a
field that currently has no approved therapies for slowing disease progression, which will be easily
translatable into clinical practice.
