c
  The care pathway template creation and adaptation processes are fully described elsewhere.1-3 In brief, clinical practice guidelines
that address SSPedi symptoms were identified systematically and a multi-disciplinary team created a care pathway template for
each SSPedi symptom based on the clinical practice guideline recommendations. The study team then worked with two clinicians at
each intervention site to adopt, adapt or reject each of 135 statements in the 14 care pathway templates (‘Headache’ and ‘Pain
other than Headache’ were combined). The draft locally adapted care pathways were finalized after distribution to wider institutional
teams for feedback.

eFigure1: Sensitivity Analyses and Approach to Missing Data

The full statistical analytic plan was uploaded to clinicaltrials.gov before analysis began.

Primary Outcome Sensitivity Analyses
1. Unadjusted Primary Outcome Model: Model with only stratification variables of number of cancer
    patients and percentage of patients with private insurance resulted in mean difference -3.7, 95% CI -
    6.3, -1.1; P=.008.
2. Square-root-transformed Outcome: Residuals were clearly non-normal, with a right-skewed
    distribution. Applying a square-root transformation led to residuals that were close to normal. Applying
    the primary fully-adjusted outcome model to the square-root transformed total SSPedi scores resulted
    in an estimated effect of -0.7, 95% CI -1.1, -0.3; P=.003.
3. Restriction to Patients Receiving Chemotherapy: To account for potential differential enrollment of
    surgery-only patients (who might not be cared for by oncology teams) between intervention and
    control sites, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients that received
    chemotherapy. The percentage of patients who received chemotherapy was 210 (95.0%) in the
    intervention group and 207 (92.4%) in the control group. Applying the primary fully-adjusted outcome
    model to this subset of patients resulted in mean difference -3.8, 95% CI -6.4, -1.2; P=.01.
4. Adjustment for Baseline SSPedi: Although the pre-specified primary analysis did not adjust for
    baseline SSPedi, we ran the primary fully-adjusted outcome model with the baseline total SSPedi
    score as an additional covariate, being cognizant that this analysis adjusted for a variable in the
    pathway between intervention and outcome, so would likely underestimate the treatment effect. The
    estimated mean difference was -3.0, 95% CI -5.2, -0.8; P=.01. Notably, there was a strong
    relationship (slope=0.5, 95% CI 0.4-0.6; P < .001) between baseline and week-8 total SSPedi scores.
5. Addressing Missing Data: Among the 445 participants, there were 15 who declined PROs and were
    enrolled as medical record review-only participants. Thus, we knew they would have missing week-8
    SSPedi scores. For the other participants, SSPedi scores were recorded using SPARK while the
    other patient-reported outcomes were recorded using REDCap. Because intervention patients were
    using SPARK for symptom screening, there were some intervention patients where the site clinical
    research associate failed to obtain the SSPedi assessment at the allotted week-4 (n=3) or week-8
    (n=4) timepoint, leading to more missing SSPedi data compared to PROMIS Fatigue or PedsQL 3.0
    Acute Cancer Module scores for intervention patients. The total numbers with missing SSPedi scores
    at the three assessment times were as follows:

      Group                          N            Baseline               Week 4                  Week 8
      Overall                       445           16 (3.6%)             30 (6.7%)               38 (8.5%)
      Symptom Screening             221            5 (2.3%)             13 (5.9%)              23 (10.4%)
      Usual Care                    224           11 (4.9%)             17 (7.6%)               15 (6.7%)

     Among all 445 participants, the overall percentage with a missing week-8 SSPedi was 8.5%, which
     was less than the 10% a priori threshold for conducting multiple imputation. When excluding medical
     record review-only participants, the amount of missing data was 23/430 (5.3%). However, since the
     worst-case performance was 10.4% missing data in the intervention group among all randomized
     participants, we conducted five different approaches to address missing week-8 SSPedi. The
     correlations between baseline and week-4 (r=0.543), baseline and week-8 (r=0.532), and week-4 and
     week-8 (r=0.709) total SSPedi scores were high. Imputation strategies were as follows:

     a) Multiple imputation with chained equations (using the mice package in R): Variables used in the
        imputation model were site (as a random effect), baseline total SSPedi score, week-4 total

        relationship with the week-8 SSPedi being missing, ethnicity, first language English or Spanish,
        preferred language English, family composition, days from diagnosis, treatment with
        chemotherapy, treatment with radiation and guardian employment status.

     b) Missing values are low: Within each site, missing values are sampled with replacement from the
        lowest 25% of observed values within the site. This presumes that all participants without week-8
        SSPedi are doing as well as the least symptomatic participants at that site.
     c) Missing values are high: Within each site, missing values are sampled with replacement from the
        highest 25% of observed values at the site. This presumes that all participants without week-8
        SSPedi are doing as poorly as the most symptomatic participants at that site.

eMethods
         The primary analysis of week-8 total Symptom Screening in Pediatrics Tool (SSPedi) score
adjusted for the stratification variables used for randomization. In addition, we adjusted for age and
diagnosis group as they are associated with total SSPedi scores.4 With the primary analysis, we did not
plan to adjust the model for baseline total SSPedi score because some of the care pathway interventions
might affect symptoms prior to enrollment and the baseline score may already reflect some benefit of the
cluster’s treatment assignment. However, sensitivity analysis adjusted for baseline total SSPedi score. To
account for potential differential enrollment of surgery-only patients (who might not be cared for by
oncology teams) between intervention vs. control sites, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to
patients that received systemic chemotherapy.
         For the analysis of documentation of symptoms, any intervention and symptom-specific
intervention, each analysis fit a mixed effects logistic regression model to estimate the odds ratio for
documentation, any intervention and symptom-specific intervention comparing intervention and control
groups, adjusting for stratification factors. We anticipated there might be too few events to reliably fit this
mixed model to all 15 symptoms. If this model could not be fit (when sigma cluster, or random effects
variance could not be estimated), we fit a fixed effects logistic regression model only using treatment
assignment.
         The primary analysis of healthcare encounters used a mixed effects Poisson regression analysis
with a random effect for site and fixed effects for the two stratification variables. Sensitivity analysis used
negative binomial regression analysis, using both frequentist and Bayesian approaches. These adjusted
for the same covariates as the Poisson regression analysis.

eResults

         There was no difference in percentages declining participation between intervention (16.6%) and
control (15.5%) sites (P=.81). However, those who agreed to participate were significantly older than
those who declined participation (median age 14.8 vs. 13.2 years, P=.001). There were no significant
differences by male sex (58.9% vs. 60.0%, P=.94) or cancer diagnosis (leukemia, lymphoma, solid tumor
or brain tumor, P=.13).
         In terms of those who were excluded due to physician preference, there was a significant
difference by intervention (16.0%) and control (5.5%) sites (P<.001). Those participating compared to
those excluded by their physician were not significantly different by age (median age 14.8 vs. 13.7 years,
P=.12) or male sex (58.9% vs. 50.9%, P=.33). However, the distribution of cancer diagnoses differed
(P<.001).
