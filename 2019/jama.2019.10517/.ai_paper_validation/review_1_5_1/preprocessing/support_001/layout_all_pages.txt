30                                                 1. PROJECT SUMMARY
31   Psychotic depression (PD) is a severe disabling disorder with considerable morbidity and mortality. Between
32   19% and 45% of inpatients with major depression have psychotic features, with greater prevalence in older
33   patients. Although electroconvulsive therapy has well-established efficacy in the treatment of PD, its use is
34   limited by several factors. As a result, the pharmacologic treatment of PD is common. Expert guidelines
35   recommend the combination of antidepressant and antipsychotic medications in the pharmacologic treatment
36   of PD. The recently completed Study of the Pharmacotherapy of Psychotic Depression (STOP-PD) was the
37   first NIMH-funded randomized controlled trial (RCT) to examine the efficacy and tolerability of newer
38   antidepressant and antipsychotic medications in the acute treatment of younger and older persons with PD.
39   The combination of sertraline and olanzapine was significantly more efficacious than olanzapine combined with
40   placebo. Both treatments were equally well tolerated, but were associated with clinically significant weight gain
41   and elevation of lipids. Older persons, however, had significantly less weight gain than younger persons.
42
43   Little is known about the continuation treatment of PD. Of particular concern, it is not known whether
44   antipsychotic medication needs to be continued once an episode of PD responds to pharmacotherapy. This
45   issue has profound clinical relevance. On the one hand, the unnecessary continuation of antipsychotic
46   medication exposes a patient to adverse effects, such as weight gain and metabolic disturbance. On the other
47   hand, premature discontinuation of antipsychotic medication has the potential risk of early relapse of a severe
48   disorder. The primary goal of this Renewal application, therefore, is to assess the risks versus benefits of
49   continuing olanzapine in younger and older patients with PD, once the episode of depression has responded to
50   treatment with sertraline and olanzapine. This goal will be addressed through a 36-week double-blind RCT, in
51   which placebo is substituted for olanzapine in half the study group, following a period of sustained remission.
52   We hypothesize that sertraline+olanzapine will be more efficacious than sertraline+placebo in preventing
53   relapse of PD. This study provides the unique opportunity to systematically assess the effect of antipsychotic
54   discontinuation (as opposed to switching from one antipsychotic to another) on olanzapine-related weight gain
55   and metabolic disturbance. Additional innovative aims of the study are to examine age and genetic
56   polymorphisms as predictors/moderators of treatment variability, potentially leading to more personalized
57   treatment of PD, and to employ population pharmacokinetics to determine the magnitude and consistency of
58   exposure to study drugs. Olanzapine is selected because it is the only atypical antipsychotic with established
59   efficacy and tolerability in the treatment of both younger and older adults with PD . This research will be
60   transformative, by providing clinicians with a much-needed evidence base to guide the continuation treatment
61   of one of the most disabling and lethal psychiatric disorders.

 85                                                     2. SPECIFIC AIMS
 86   2.1 INTRODUCTION TO STUDY AIMS: Major depression with psychotic features (psychotic depression; PD)
 87   is a severe disorder associated with considerable morbidity and mortality.1 Although ECT has well-established
 88   efficacy as a treatment for PD,2,3 its use is limited by several factors: a high rate of relapse following completion
 89   of ECT,4,5 lower rates of response in non-academic centers,6 significant disparities in its availability,7 and the
 90   fact that many patients prefer pharmacologic treatment, because they find the concept of ECT and the
 91   potential side effect of cognitive impairment unacceptable. Because of these limitations, the pharmacologic
 92   treatment of PD is common.8 Expert guidelines recommend the combination of antidepressant and
 93   antipsychotic medications as an alternative to ECT in the treatment of PD.9,10 The recently completed Study of
 94   the Pharmacotherapy of Psychotic Depression (STOP-PD)11, which involved collaboration of the current group
 95   of principal investigators, was the first NIMH-funded RCT to examine the efficacy and tolerability of
 96   combination therapy using newer antidepressant and antipsychotic medications in the acute treatment of PD.
 97   The combination of sertraline and olanzapine was significantly more efficacious than olanzapine monotherapy,
 98   and the two treatments had comparable tolerability.11 Nevertheless, both treatments were associated with
 99   increases in weight and lipids over the 12-week study.11
100        Once an episode of major depression responds to antidepressant medication, the antidepressant must be
101   continued to prevent relapse.12 However, it is not known whether antipsychotic medication needs to be
102   continued once an episode of PD has responded to combined antidepressant-antipsychotic treatment. This
103   issue is of profound clinical importance. On the one hand, the unnecessary continuation of antipsychotic
104   medication exposes a patient to adverse effects, some of which have considerable public health significance.
105   On the other hand, premature discontinuation of antipsychotic medication has the potential risk of early relapse
106   of a severe, disabling disorder. The main goal of this collaborative Renewal Application, therefore, is to assess
107   the benefits and risks of continuing olanzapine in older and younger persons with PD, once the episode of
108   depression has responded to treatment with sertraline and olanzapine. This goal will be addressed through a
109   RCT, in which placebo is substituted for olanzapine in half the study group, following a period of sustained
110   remission. Specifically, we propose a three-phase study. In the Acute Phase, participants will receive open-
111   label sertraline and olanzapine for a maximum of 12 weeks. Subjects who respond to treatment will enter the
112   Stabilization Phase, where they will continue to receive open-label sertraline and olanzapine for an additional 8
113   weeks. Participants who meet remission criteria will then be randomized to 36 weeks of double-blind treatment
114   with either sertraline+olanzapine or sertraline+placebo.
115
116   2.2 PRIMARY AIM
117   Aim 1: Assess the efficacy of olanzapine, in combination with sertraline, in preventing relapse of
118   psychotic depression
119   H1: The combination of sertraline and olanzapine will be associated with less risk of relapse than the
120   combination of sertraline and placebo
121
122   2.3. SECONDARY AIMS
123   Aim 2: Assess the effect of continuation versus discontinuation of olanzapine on weight and metabolic
124   variables in remitted psychotic depression
125   H2: The combination of sertraline and olanzapine will be associated with higher weight, higher total cholesterol,
126   and higher triglycerides compared with the combination of sertraline and placebo in the randomized phase
127   Aim 3: Assess the effect of age on olanzapine–associated weight gain in the acute and stabilization
128   treatment of psychotic depression (open-label phase of the study)
129   H3: Older age will be associated with less weight gain during the open-label phase
130
131   2.4 EXPLORATORY AIMS
132   Aim 4. Explore older age as a moderator of change in weight and metabolic variables during the
133   randomized phase
134   Aim 5. Explore the association of selected genetic polymorphisms with: i) response, ii) relapse, and iii)
135   weight and metabolic variables during the open-label and randomized phases of the study

140                                                3. RESEARCH STRATEGY
141                                                     SIGNIFICANCE
142   3.1 PD is a severe, disabling disorder with considerable morbidity and mortality.1,13 15-20% of persons with
143   major depression have a lifetime history of psychotic features.14,15 19-45% of inpatients with major depression
144   have psychosis, with older depressed persons at greatest risk of PD.16,17 PD has been associated with more
145   severe symptoms, slower recovery, greater risk of relapse and recurrence, greater frequency of psychiatric
146   hospitalizations, more psychosocial impairment, higher all-cause mortality, and (in some studies) a higher
147   frequency of suicide than non-psychotic depression (non-PD).13,18-21
148   3.2 The Acute Pharmacologic Treatment of PD. Several studies have found that PD has better response to
149   a combination of antidepressant and antipsychotic medications, than to either medication alone.11 For the most
150   part, these studies used older generation antidepressant and antipsychotic medications. These findings
151   resulted in practice guidelines recommending combination treatment as the pharmacologic treatment of choice
152   for the acute treatment of PD.9,10 Following the availability of SSRI and SNRI antidepressants, a single group of
153   investigators reported a high rate of response of PD to monotherapy with SSRIs and venlafaxine, based on 3
154   separate RCTs.22-24 The findings of these studies are, however, limited by the fact that none included a
155   placebo-control group, and they lacked a validated measure of psychosis, raising the possibility that persons
156   with non-delusional ideation may have been inadvertently included in the studies.25 Also, these findings have
157   not been replicated in studies by other groups of investigators.26-28 Thus, the efficacy of SSRI and SNRI
158   monotherapy to treat PD is uncertain, and combined antidepressant-antipsychotic medication remains the
159   pharmacologic treatment for PD that is recommended by expert guidelines.
160   3.3 Longer Term Treatment of PD: The Conundrum. Little is known about the continuation and maintenance
161   treatment of PD. Once an episode of major depression responds to antidepressant medication, the
162   antidepressant needs to be continued to prevent relapse and recurrence of depression.12,29,30 However, it is not
163   known whether antipsychotic medication needs to be continued once an episode of PD has responded to
164   combined antidepressant-antipsychotic treatment. On the one hand, premature discontinuation of
165   antipsychotic medication has the potential risk of relapse of a severe, disabling disorder. On the other hand,
166   the unnecessary continuation of antipsychotic medication exposes a patient to potential adverse effects. The
167   two sides of this very significant clinical conundrum will now be discussed.
168   3.4 Relapse of PD. Naturalistic observational studies of PD report relapse rates of 17%-50% (mean=30%)
169   over 12-months of follow-up.31-36 Studies that have specifically examined the frequency of relapse of PD
170   following a course of ECT have, with one exception, found 12-month rates of 35-50%, despite adequate
171   continuation pharmacotherapy.37-40 The high rate of relapse after the discontinuation of ECT, and the lack of
172   clarity regarding optimal continuation treatment following ECT, underscores the importance of examining
173   pharmacologic approaches to the longer-term treatment of PD.
174            There are few data on the relation between discontinuation of antipsychotic medication and relapse of
175   PD. In a chart review of persons with PD who had responded to pharmacotherapy, Aronson et al.32 found that
176   approximately 35% of relapses occurred following the discontinuation of antipsychotic medication in persons
177   who continued to take antidepressant medication; the 3 months after antipsychotic discontinuation was the
178   period of greatest risk of relapse. The only prospective study of antipsychotic discontinuation in PD found a
179   frequency of relapse of 27% over 8 months in 30 mid-life adults who had recovered with combined fluoxetine
180   and perphenazine;41 the findings of this single study are, however, limited by its open and uncontrolled design,
181   the relatively small number of patients, and the absence of older patients who, based on studies of non-PD,42
182   may conceivably be at greater risk of relapse.
183            Finally, a few uncontrolled studies have examined relapse in persons with PD who are maintained on
184   the treatment to which they responded.39,43,44 In contrast with the aforementioned findings, relapse rates in
185   these studies range from only 0% to 6% over 12 months. Although the findings of these studies are limited by
186   relatively small numbers of patients and uncontrolled design, they suggest a low risk of relapse in patients with
187   PD who are maintained on the treatment regimen that was associated with response.
188   3.5 Atypical Antipsychotics in the Treatment of PD: Choice of Olanzapine. Atypical antipsychotics have
189   become the first-line treatment of psychosis. Olanzapine is the only atypical with RCT-evidence of efficacy and
190   tolerability in the treatment of both younger and older persons with PD.11,45 In STOP-PD, only 5% of
191   participants withdrew from the 12-week study because of side effects.1 Nevertheless, olanzapine is associated
192   with weight gain and metabolic effects.11,46 Our choice of olanzapine for the proposed study is based on careful
193   consideration of alternative atypical antipsychotics that have less severe metabolic effects but do not have
194   documented efficacy for the treatment of PD. Without evidence, efficacy of other atypical antipsychotics in PD

                                                                                                                     4
195   cannot be assumed; for example, olanzapine has been found to be more effective than other atypicals in
196   several RCTs of the treatment of schizophrenia.47-51 Each atypical antipsychotic has a number of adverse
197   effects, some of which are potentially problematic in the treatment of PD, especially in older patients. 52-54
198   Although aripiprazole and ziprasidone have a low incidence of clinically significant weight gain and metabolic
199   dysregulation,52,53 there are few data on the efficacy, tolerability, and safety of these drugs in older persons.54
200   Ziprasidone’s potential for QTc interval prolongation is a potential concern in older persons with cardiac
201   disease54 and would potentially result in the exclusion of many older patients who would otherwise be eligible
202   for the study. Both ziprasidone and aripiprazole are associated with activation and insomnia,52-54 and in the
203   case of aripirazole, akathisia and nausea.52-54 In a recent open-label pilot study of combined aripiprazole and
204   citalopram in 16 younger adults with PD, 63% of participants developed akathisia on a mean daily aripiprazole
205   dose of 14.4 ± 5.5 mg.55 The tolerability of aripiprazole and ziprasidone in persons with agitation (a frequent
206   symptom of PD) is unclear. In addition, patients with PD are at high risk of attempted and completed
207   suicide.20,21 It is unknown whether the activating effects of aripiprazole and ziprasidone, especially when given
208   in combination with an SSRI, would increase the risk of suicidality in persons with PD. In light of the efficacy of
209   the sertraline/olanzapine combination in STOP-PD,11 and the good overall tolerability and safety of this
210   treatment,11 we have chosen to continue to study this regimen rather than simply assuming that a different
211   antidepressant-antipsychotic combination would be comparably efficacious and better tolerated in younger and
212   older patients. Finally, olanzapine accounts for 25% of sales of atypical antipsychotics in the United States and
213   30% of sales worldwide.56 These figures have remained constant over the past 4 years.56 This ongoing,
214   widespread use of olanzapine, despite its known metabolic effects, underscores the importance of the
215   proposed study, to provide patients and clinicians with the best available evidence about the risks and benefits
216   of discontinuing this medication in the long-term treatment of PD.
217   3.6 Personalizing the Treatment of PD: Pharmacogenetic Considerations. As with other psychiatric
218   disorders, the pharmacologic treatment of PD is associated with considerable treatment variability.8
219   Pharmacogenetic findings have the potential to result in more personalized treatment and possibly preventive
220   strategies. Because the number of individuals recruited to a clinical trial is insufficient to conduct a genome-
221   wide association study,57 we will use a candidate-gene approach to explore the following important issues:
222   1) Genetic predictors of relapse of either mood disorders or primary psychotic disorders are currently unknown.
223   We will therefore explore this issue in relation to the proposed study. We assume that the withdrawal of
224   olanzapine will remove pharmacologic antagonism of principally D2 and 5HT2A postsynaptic receptors; the
225   synaptic system will then adapt using autoreceptor mechanisms that might include 5HT1A. 58-60 We therefore
226   hypothesize that the genes producing these receptors will be of primary interest as potentially involved in
227   relapse of PD following discontinuation of olanzapine. Of note, the 5HT1A -1019 C/G polymorphism has been
228   implicated in vulnerability to both depression and psychosis,61,62 and the Taq1A and -141ins/del polymorphisms
229   of the D2 gene have been associated with vulnerability to psychosis.63
230   2) Several genetic polymorphisms (5HT2C -759C/T, 5HTTLPR s allele, SNAP 25, Leptin -2584 A/G, ADR2a -
231   1291C/G, INSIG2) have been associated with antipsychotic-related weight gain.64-77 However, it is not known
232   whether these polymorphisms predict reversal of weight gain following discontinuation of the antipsychotic. We
233   will explore this important issue.
234   3) There have been no published studies of the pharmacogenetics of response or remission in PD. We will
235   therefore explore whether genetic polymorphisms that have been associated with antidepressant response in
236   non-PD (5HTTLPR s and lg alleles, 5HT2A -1438G/A, FKBP5, CRHR1)78-86 and antipsychotic response (D2 -
237   141 ins/del and Taq1A, D3 ser9gly, SNAP 25) and weight gain in schizophrenia64-77,87-93 pertain to PD.
238            Finally, it is important to note that we will participate in the NIMH Human Genetics Initiative so that cell
239   lines, DNA, and genetic data from this very well characterized sample will be available to the wider scientific
240   community. For example, there is emerging evidence that certain genes (e.g.DISC1,94 CACNA1C95)may confer
241   risk for severe psychiatric illness, as opposed to specific disorders. The genetic data from our study will provide
242   scientists with the unique opportunity to include persons with psychotic depression in analyses pertaining to
243   these ‘susceptibility genes’.
244                                                         INNOVATION
245    3.7 In addition to being transformative, the proposed research is innovative in several important ways:
246   • By focusing on PD, this study provides the unique opportunity to assess the effect of antipsychotic
247   discontinuation (as opposed to switching from one antipsychotic to another, which is the case in studies of
248   schizophrenia) on treatment-associated weight gain and metabolic disturbance;


                                                                                                                         5
249   • The study is unique in its inclusion of both younger and older adults. This novel approach will allow us to
250   address age-related questions pertaining to treatment variability;
251   • An innovative aspect of the design is to include persons in the RCT who have achieved ‘near-remission’ of
252   depressive symptoms. This will allow us to examine whether substantial, but not complete, resolution of
253   depressive symptoms in PD increases the risk of relapse following discontinuation of antipsychotic medication,
254   • Studying genetic predictors of relapse of PD will be highly innovative. Moreover, this will be the first study to
255   examine whether genetic polymorphisms that predict antipsychotic-related weight gain also predict weight loss
256   following antipsychotic discontinuation;
257   • Population pharmacokinetics will be used to evaluate drug exposure, which will allow for a more informed and
258   sophisticated interpretation of the study’s findings;
259   • This study will continue work started in STOP-PD in developing innovative assessment instruments, such as
260   the Delusion Assessment Scale,96 in this population. During the course of STOP-PD, we developed the
261   Resolution of Delusions Scale (RODS) to evaluate change in a person’s insight, as a delusion resolves with
262   treatment. In the proposed study, we will assess the psychometric properties of the RODS. If metrics are
263   acceptable, we will use the RODS in exploratory analyses to determine to what extent insight into the prior
264   delusional belief is associated with stability of remission and risk of relapse;
265   • A novel feature of the assessment is the use of a behavioral sign-based measure of psychomotor function
266   (the ‘CORE’97) to examine the effect of psychomotor change, which is common in PD, on treatment outcome;
267   • If the proposed study is funded, we are committed to promptly submitting a competing supplement that will
268   have a cognitive neuroscience focus, integrating neuropsychology and neuroimaging, to study the relationships
269   between age, brain function, and course of PD.
270                                                        APPROACH
271   3.8 PROGRESS REPORT ON STOP-PD (Dates of Award: 6/1/2002-5/31/2007).
272   STOP-PD was a 12-week double-blind RCT that compared the efficacy, tolerability, and safety of olanzapine
273   plus sertraline (‘combination therapy’) with olanzapine plus placebo (‘olanzapine monotherapy’) in younger and
274   older persons with major depressive disorder and delusions ± hallucinations. The study addressed the
275   following hypotheses: 1) combined olanzapine and sertraline would be more efficacious than combined
276   olanzapine and placebo; 2) the younger age group would have a higher rate of remission than the older age
277   group; 3) treatment would be less well tolerated by older than younger participants. Full details of this study are
278   reported in Arch Gen Psychiatry 2009;66 (8):838-47.11 To summarize:
279   • The study’s recruitment goal was met, with 259 patients randomized to treatment (n=142 ≥60 years and
280   n=117 <60 years) (Cornell: n=56; Massachusetts: n=59; Pittsburgh: n=63; Toronto: n=81).
281   • Combination therapy was associated with a higher remission rate over the trial than olanzapine monotherapy
282   (OR=1.28, 95% CI =1.12-1.47, p<.001). 41.9% of randomized combination participants were in remission at
283   their last assessment compared to 23.9% of olanzapine monotherapy participants (21= 9.53, p=0.002;
284   NNT=5.6). Of the participants who completed 12 weeks of treatment, the remission rate was 66.7% with
285   combined treatment versus 49.2% with monotherapy (χ21=4.4, p=0.036; NNT=5.7).
286   • Younger and older participants did not differ in rate of remission (OR=1.05, 95% CI=0.80-1.37, p=0.75).
287   • There was no significant difference between age groups in overall tolerability of treatment or in
288   extrapyramidal side effect scores. Both age groups had statistically significant increases in weight, cholesterol,
289   and triglycerides. Of note, older subjects had significantly less weight gain than younger subjects (7.3 ± 10.3
290   lbs versus 13.9 ± 4.0 lbs respectively; F1,226=14.51, p=0.0002). A statistically significant increase in fasting
291   plasma glucose levels occurred in younger subjects only (mean change of 8.4 ± 41.3 mg/dL from a mean
292   baseline of 93.6 ± 20.4 mg/dL, t211=2.65, p=0.009). Only 5% of participants withdrew from the study because of
293   adverse effects.
294   Unpublished Pilot Data from STOP-PD that is Relevant to the Proposed Study:
295   • As an unfunded pilot project, blood was obtained at week 12/termination in a sub-set of participants (n=66
296   young and n=102 old) for future pharmacokinetic analyses. Since younger and older groups differed in weight
297   gain, we used population pharmacokinetics to explore whether younger and older groups differed in: i)
298   variability of exposure, which could indicate poorer adherence with olanzapine in one group versus the other
299   [105,106], and ii) magnitude of exposure. Younger and older groups did not differ in either variability
300   (0.98±0.27 vs. 0.96±0.30; t[166]=0.36, p=0.72) or magnitude (1128.9±737.6ug/mL*h vs.1027.1±477.6ug/mL*h;
301   unequal variances t[102]=1.0, p=.32) of exposure, suggesting that the age-related difference in weight gain was
302   not explained by exposure. The proposed study will examine drug exposure in all participants.

                                                                                                                       6
303   • Participants who were in remission with either combination therapy or olanzapine monotherapy at the
304   completion of STOP-PD were invited to participate in a 3-month double-blind stabilization phase, during which
305   they continued their acute phase treatment. The stabilization phase was designed to collect pilot data on: i) the
306   stability of remission, and ii) the course of side effects. 74 persons (n=29 young and n=45 old) participated in
307   this phase. Of the 48 patients who experienced remission with combination treatment, 10.4% subsequently
308   had a relapse of major depression, psychosis, or both. Participants continued to gain weight in the stabilization
309   phase, but at a slower rate than during STOP-PD (approximately 1.5 lbs/month in stabilization, compared with
310   4.4 lbs/month in STOP-PD). Tests of fixed effects for changes in metabolic variables during the 6 months of
311   both STOP-PD and the stabilization phase found a statistically significant age x time effect for glucose (F3,149=
312   3.16, p<0.03), and trends in age x time effects for triglycerides (p=0.08), total cholesterol (p=0.07), and LDL
313   cholesterol (p=0.09). The younger remitters experienced more of an increase in these metabolic variables than
314   the older remitters. Thus, whereas there was no significant difference between age groups in elevations of
315   glucose and lipids by the end of STOP-PD, differences started to emerge after 6 months of treatment. This
316   suggests that older age may moderate olanzapine-associated metabolic dysfunction, but that this effect may
317   not be apparent until later in the course of treatment. Thus, Aim 3, which pertains to the acute/stabilization
318   phases of treatment (which the majority of participants are expected to complete within 16 weeks),
319   hypothesizes that older age will moderate weight gain only, whereas Aim 4, which pertains to the longer-term
320   treatment of PD, explores whether older age moderates change in both weight and metabolic variables.

355   study’s findings by recruiting persons from ‘less academically-oriented settings’. Entry criteria (see Section
356   4.2.1) will be the same as those used in STOP-PD which achieved its recruitment goal. Given that the acute
357   phase of the proposed study will use open-label combination treatment, we anticipate that more patients will
358   agree to participate in the proposed study compared with STOP-PD where participants had a 50:50 chance of
359   being randomized to olanzapine plus placebo. Based on 38 months of recruitment, the recruitment goal
360   equates to an average 2.6 participants enrolled per site per month.
361   Enrollment in the Stabilization and RCT Phases: In the double-blind combination treatment arm of STOP-
362   PD, 63/129 (48.8%) participants who were randomized to combination treatment met remission criteria at
363   some point during treatment, based on the criterion of a Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia
364   (SADS)101 delusion item score of 1 (‘definitely not delusional’) and a minimum of 2 consecutive assessments of
365   a 17-item HAM-D score ≤10. In addition, 10/129 (7.8%) combination treatment participants had the absence of
366   delusions, a CGI improvement score of ‘very much’ or ‘much’ improved, and a 17-item HAM-D score of 11-15.
367   Thus, 56.6% of participants who were randomized to combination treatment experienced remission of
368   delusions and remission or substantial improvement of depressive symptoms. Given that treatment in the
369   acute phase of the proposed study will be open-label, we anticipate that remission/near-remission will be
370   higher and drop-out due to withdrawal of consent will be lower than under the double-blind, placebo-controlled
371   conditions of STOP-PD.11 We therefore predict that 60% of participants in the proposed study will achieve
372   remission of psychosis and either remission or substantial improvement in depressive symptoms, allowing
373   them to proceed to the stabilization phase. Nevertheless, we plan to recruit 392 patients to the acute phase of
374   the study, which will facilitate reaching our target recruitment to the RCT should remission plus near-remission
375   in the acute phase be as low as 50%.
376           In the STOP-PD stabilization phase, 10% of participants who experienced remission with combination
377   treatment had a relapse (Section 3.8). In contrast, Rothschild et al.41 found that none of the patients with PD
378   who responded to combined fluoxetine and perphenazine had a relapse or dropped out during 12 weeks of
379   open-label continuation treatment. Based on these data, we estimate that approximately 10% of acute phase
380   full/near-remitters will not complete the 8-week open-label stabilization phase of the proposed study, either
381   because of relapse or discontinuation. Thus, we predict that 176 participants will be available for the RCT.
382   3.9.3 Justification for Aspects of the Design:
383   Duration of Acute Treatment: Although most studies of the acute treatment of PD have been of 6-8 weeks
384   duration,8 a maximum of 12 weeks of acute treatment is more suitable for this study for several reasons. First,
385   one of the goals of the acute phase is to maximize the chance of achieving remission of delusions and
386   antidepressant response, in order for participants to enter the RCT. In STOP-PD, 1/3 of participants who
387   experienced remission with combined sertraline and olanzapine did not do so until between weeks 6 and 12 of
388   treatment. Second, studies of 6-8 weeks typically use one cross-sectional assessment of ≥50% decrease in
389   severity of depression as the primary outcome criterion. The acute treatment outcome criterion that will be
390   used in this study requires a longer period of observation. On the other hand, the decision to limit acute
                                                                                                                     8
391   treatment to a maximum of 12 weeks is based on study time frame considerations (completion of the study in 5
392   years) and the fact that patients who do not experience remission of psychosis and at least substantial
393   improvement in depression following 12 weeks of intensive antidepressant-antipsychotic treatment should
394   probably be considered for additional or alternative treatment.
395   Duration of Stabilization Phase: There are no data, and there is no consensus among experts,102 about how
396   long to continue antipsychotic medication, once an episode of PD has responded to combination
397   pharmacotherapy. However, a survey of 50 experts in the treatment of late-life depression found that the
398   majority would not recommend stopping antipsychotic medication immediately after remission of PD.102 In the
399   absence of data to the contrary, we believe that it is prudent to allow for a period of stability of remission of
400   psychosis prior to withdrawal of the antipsychotic. On the other hand, continuation of the antipsychotic for too
401   long exposes a patient to the hazards of ongoing weight gain and metabolic dysfunction. We therefore chose 8
402   weeks for the stabilization period to strike a balance between assuring sustained remission of the psychosis,
403   while minimizing the duration of exposure to weight gain and metabolic disturbance.
404   Discontinuation Design: The primary aim of this study is to assess whether olanzapine, in combination with
405   antidepressant medication, prevents relapse of PD. This question can be addressed through a double-blind
406   RCT, in which placebo is substituted for olanzapine in half the study participants, at a fixed point in time. We
407   also considered a placebo-controlled sequential discontinuation design, whereby placebo is substituted for
408   olanzapine at different time points. Although a sequential discontinuation design could provide information
409   about how long to continue olanzapine once an episode of PD has remitted, we believe that the first step is to
410   determine whether or not antipsychotic medication is of any benefit in preventing relapse of PD. If antipsychotic
411   medication is found to be beneficial, the next step would then be to assess the optimal duration of
412   antipsychotic treatment. A sequential discontinuation design would require many more study participants than
413   the design that we propose, and would therefore be more challenging in terms of recruitment and more costly
414   than the current proposal.
415   Duration of the RCT: Limited data from naturalistic studies of PD suggest that when antipsychotic medication
416   is discontinued, the 3 months after discontinuation is the period of greatest risk of relapse.32,41 The choice of 36
417   weeks for the proposed RCT therefore: a) covers the period of greatest risk of relapse of PD following the
418   discontinuation of the antipsychotic, yet allows for a period of observation beyond that time, b) saves on the
419   cost that would be associated with a longer study, and c) allows the study to be completed within 5 years.
420   Duration of Antipsychotic Taper: In the aformentioned open-label study of the withdrawal of antipsychotic
421   medication in PD, Rothschild et al.41 tapered antipsychotic medication over a period of 4 weeks. No patient
422   experienced discontinuation side effects or re-emergence of psychosis during the 4-week taper.
423   Stratification by Age: PD is prevalent in older adults with major depression.16,17,103,104 Age-related
424   pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic changes may result in age-related differences in treatment efficacy
425   and tolerability. It is therefore important to include a sufficient number of older persons in order to examine the
426   relation between age, efficacy, tolerability, and safety.
427   Remission Criteria to Enter the RCT: Studies of major depression have found that partial improvement in
428   depressive symptoms may increase the risk of subsequent relapse of depression.105 We therefore believe
429   that, in addition to experiencing full remission of psychosis, participants should experience substantial
430   improvement in depressive symptoms prior to changes being made to the treatment to which they responded.
431   There were patients in STOP-PD who experienced remission of psychosis and were rated by a research
432   psychiatrist as ‘very much improved’ or ‘much improved’ on the CGI, yet had a final visit HAM-D score in the
433   range of 11-15, which still represented ≥50% improvement in the baseline HAM-D score. Given that patients in
434   this study will continue with an antidepressant throughout the RCT, and may therefore continue to experience
435   improvement in depression beyond the acute/stabilization phases of treatment, we will include persons with
436   substantial improvement in depressive symptoms, even though the symptoms may not have reached the
437   conventional < 8-10 HAM-D cut point for remission used in studies of non-psychotic depression.29,30
438   MMSE Score ≥24 to Enter the Stabilization Phase: Persons with clinically significant cognitive impairment
439   prior to the index episode of PD will be excluded from the study (Section 4.2.1). In addition, participants with a
440   MMSE score <24 at the end of the stabilization phase will be excluded from participation in the RCT (since
441   poor cognitive performance at that stage of the study can not be explained by depression or psychosis),unless
442   a MMSE score of <24 is explained by factors other than cognitive impairment, such as low education level,
443   visual impairment, or lack of fluency in the English language.Of the STOP-PD participants who achieved
444   remission with combination treatment and participated in the stabilization phase, only 1/37 had a MMSE score


                                                                                                                        9
445   <24 at the end of stabilization. Thus, we expect that this exclusion will have minimal impact on subject flow in
446   to the RCT.

541   3.9.7 Drug Exposure: Pharmacokinetic changes associated with aging and other factors134,135 can result in
542   variability of drug concentrations, which in turn can result in differences in pharmacodynamics. Drug exposure
543   is therefore an important piece of information when interpreting variability in treatment efficacy and adverse
544   effects.134,136 Blood will be collected for determination of plasma sertraline and olanzapine concentrations.
545   Each site will send frozen batched plasma samples to Dr. Pollock’s psychopharmacology lab in Toronto every
546   6 months for pharmacokinetic analyses. The analytic strategy will use population pharmacokinetics which uses
547   nonlinear mixed effect modeling137 to identify intra- and inter-individual sources of variability. The modeling
548   provides: (1) magnitude of exposure, expressed as average drug concentration or Area Under the Curve, and
549   (2) variability of exposure from what is expected, expressed as a ratio of the predicted drug concentration for a
550   given time point to the observed concentration (Cpred/obs). Variability from the norm in drug concentrations
551   can be determined using sparse (between two and four) plasma samples per patient.138
552   3.9.8 Outcomes
553   Outcome Criteria: Relapse is the primary measure of outcome. Relapse criteria are broad, to reflect a range
554   of clinically relevant outcomes of PD. The criterion for relapse will be at least one of the following: 1) SCID
555   symptoms of major depressive episode (preferably maintained for at least 1 week over two consecutive weekly
556   visits); 2) 17-item HAM-D score of ≥18 (preferably maintained for at least 1 week over two consecutive weekly
557   visits) and a mean absolute increase of 5 points on at least one visit relative to entry into the RCT; 3) re-
558   emergence of SCID-rated psychosis (delusions or hallucinations) (preferably for at least 1 week over two
559   consecutive weekly visits) and a score of ≥3 on the SADS delusion or hallucination severity items
560   (delusion/hallucination ‘definitely present’); 4) significant clinical worsening, defined as either i) emergence of
561   ‘high risk’ of suicide at any time (current suicide plan or suicide attempt), and/or ii) development of SCID-rated
562   mania or hypomania (preferably for at least 1 week over two consecutive weekly visits), and/or iii) psychiatric
563   hospitalization for depression, psychosis, suicidality, or mania/hypomania, regardless of the duration of these
564   symptoms. Although a one-week time duration is preferable, the research psychiatrist is allowed to declare that
565   a relapse has occurred if the clinical criterion is established at an in-person visit and postponing further
566   intervention for another seven days is considered to place the subject at undue risk. Time to relapse will be
567   defined as time from randomization to the first visit at which relapse is established or the start of psychiatric
568   hospitalization. These relapse criteria reflect a clinical deterioration for which most clinicians would consider a
569   change of treatment. Even though patients with a history of bipolar affective disorder will not be allowed to
570   enter the study (Section 4.2.1), mania or hypomania is included as an outcome variable because onset of PD
571   in younger adults may predict subsequent development of bipolar affective disorder. Participants who are
572   suspected of having a relapse will be seen by one of the study’s research psychiatrists who will determine
573   whether relapse criteria are met.
574   Management of Relapse: Patients who meet relapse criteria will be seen for clinical management by a
575   psychiatrist at the research site who is not affiliated with the study within 48-72 hours of referral. Alternatively,
576   patients can be referred for acute psychiatric hospitalization, if deemed appropriate by the study psychiatrist.
577   The non-study psychiatrist will be notified by the research pharmacist whether the patient was taking
578   olanzapine at the time of relapse. Other research personnel will not be aware of the treatment assignment.
579   Based on the frequency of relapse postulated for the study, we estimate that there will be an average of 3
580   cases of relapse per year per study site.

622   will be carefully recorded and will be reported in a descriptive fashion for each treatment arm.

624   3.10 DATA ANALYSIS
625   3.10.1 Data Management is described as a part of Quality Control in Section 4.1.6.
626   3.10.2 Randomization: We will randomize 88 participants to each of the two treatment arms using a 1:1
627   allocation ratio, stratified by age, remission group at randomization, and site. In order to reduce the probability
628   that a disproportionate number of subjects are randomized to any one level of the factors a blocking strategy
629   will be used. Randomization will be conducted by Dr. Leon, the study’s statistician, using the Dallal software.
630   3.10.3 Data Analytic Procedures: Initially, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics will be
631   examined. Frequency distributions will be produced. Descriptive and graphical displays will be produced.
632   Transformations will be used when distributional assumptions are not fulfilled for inferential tests. Treatment
633   groups will be compared on baseline demographic and clinical variables using t-tests for continuous variables,
634   Mann-Whitney tests for ordinal variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. If significant baseline
635   group imbalance is detected on any particular variable, that variable will be included as a covariate in the
636   inferential analyses if its correlation with outcome is 0.30 or greater. Dropouts and completers will also be
637   compared on baseline variables using t-tests, Mann-Whitney tests, or chi-square tests. The primary analyses
638   will adhere to the Intent-to-Treat principle. Each statistical test for H1-3 will involve a two-tailed alpha of .05.
639   Primary Hypothesis: Efficacy.
640   H1: The combination of sertraline and olanzapine will be associated with less risk of relapse than the
641   combination of sertraline and placebo. H1 will be tested with a Cox proportional hazards model that
642   compares survival time across treatment groups. Survival time will be defined as time from randomization to
643   relapse (Section 3.9.8) and will be measured in weeks. A subject’s survival time will be classified as censored
644   at the point of study discontinuation, (e.g., due to withdrawal of consent or a severe intervening non-psychiatric
645   medical event) or at end of follow-up, if relapse has not yet occurred. Kaplan Meier survival curves will be used

                                                                                                                        13
646   for descriptive analyses of time to relapse. The Cox models will include treatment group and the stratification
647   variables: site, remission group at randomization (full-remission vs. near-remission), and age group at
648   randomization (18-59 vs. ≥60 years). Treatment groups will be compared on 5 variables at the time of
649   randomization that have the potential to be associated with risk of relapse (anxiety, medical burden, executive
650   dysfunction, psychomotor change, and treatment resistance prior to study entry; Section 3.9.5); if the treatment
651   groups differ significantly on one or more of these variables (p≤.05) and the variable in question is correlated
652   with relapse (r>.30), the variable will be included as a covariate in the Cox model. Based on our prior
653   studies,38,41,139 we expect no more than 10% attrition during the RCT among these participants who have
654   already been enrolled for 12-20 weeks prior to randomization and who are in sustained remission at the time of
655   randomization. This attrition rate might appear to be low, but this is because those subjects most vulnerable to
656   attrition will likely drop out in the phases prior to randomization. Non-informative censoring will be examined by
657   comparing those who do and do not dropout on their HAM-D ratings from the prior week. The constant hazards
658   assumption will be examined by examining the incremental contribution of a treatment x vulnerable period of
659   relapse interaction (exploring the possibility of higher risk in the first 3 months following discontinuation).
660   Secondary Hypotheses: Weight and Metabolic Safety
661   H2: The combination of sertraline and olanzapine will be associated with higher weight, higher total
662   cholesterol, and higher triglycerides compared with the combination of sertraline and placebo in the
663   randomized phase. This will be tested using separate mixed-effects linear regression analyses for weight,
664   cholesterol, and triglycerides during the randomized phase. Models will include up to 15 repeated
665   assessments of weight and up to 5 repeated measures of total cholesterol and triglycerides over the 36-week
666   trial, as the respective dependent variables. Using a regulatory approach to safety analyses (H2, H3, E1 and
667   E2), we will classify subjects based on medications taken during protocolized treatment. Likelihood ratio (LR)
668   tests will successively examine the incremental contributions of a quadratic term for time and two interactions
669   (treatment by site and treatment by time).140 The decision rule calls for rejection of H0 if the latter interaction is
670   statistically significant. Interaction and quadratic terms will only be included in subsequent models if statistically
671   significant. LR tests will determine if the time better approximated with weekly time indicators that allow for
672   other forms of non-linear change over time. In addition a spline-based approach will examine metabolic
673   changes. If a participant receives new treatment or a change of treatment for hyperlipidemia during the RCT,
674   his/her pertinent metabolic data from that point onwards will be excluded from the mixed model, although the
675   metabolic measures in question will continue to be collected for safety analyses and reporting to the DSMB.
676   Based on data from STOP-PD, we expect that fewer than 5% of participants (i.e. <10 participants in the RCT)
677   will have metabolic data censored for this reason. We chose not to include these post-randomization metabolic
678   data as covariates in the mixed model, because doing so could confound ‘cause and effect’ and result in a
679   biased estimate of the treatment effect, thereby compromising the primary goal of the analysis.141
680   H3: Older age will be associated with less weight gain during the open-label phase. Mixed-effects linear
681   regression analysis will examine weight change from Acute Phase baseline. The model will include up to 6
682   weight assessments over the 12-20 week acute and stabilization phases. We will use the strategy above (H2)
683   except that age will be the primary fixed effect because treatment is a constant in the open-label phase. LR
684   tests will examine incremental contributions of a quadratic term for time and the age by time interaction. In
685   addition a spline-based approach will be used to fit metabolic measures. H0 will be rejected if the interaction is
686   statistically significant.
687   Exploratory Analyses
688   E1. Explore older age as a moderator of change in weight and metabolic variables during the
689   randomized phase. Analyses of age as a moderator are exploratory and, as recommended142 will focus on
690   the magnitude of the effect and not involve significance testing. Separate mixed-effects linear regression
691   models will examine change on weight, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and insulin resistance.
692   E2. Explore the association of selected genetic polymorphisms with: i) remission, ii) relapse, and iii)
693   weight and metabolic variables during the open-label and randomized phases of the study. We will
694   apply quality control for the genotyped genetic markers: i) all tag SNPs with a call rate less than 95% and
695   minor allele frequency less than 10% will be excluded to provide sufficiently informative variation, and ii) we will
696   test for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) for each marker; markers not in HWE will be discarded (p<.001).
697   The analyses for remission and relapse will compare those with and without each candidate genotype on time
698   until remission/relapse in separate Cox proportional hazards models. Analyses will examine one genotype per
699   model. Mixed-effects linear regression analyses will compare those with and without each candidate genotype
700   on weight and metabolic variables. There will be separate models for weight and each pertinent metabolic

701   variable; these analyses will examine one genotype per model. Each open-label analysis will involve a main
702   effect of genotype, whereas each randomized phase analysis will involve both the main effects and interaction
703   of genotype and randomized treatment. Haplotype analyses will follow the same approach as for genotype
704   analyses. Haplotypes occurring at a frequency of <10% will not be included in analyses.
705   Analysis of Non-Metabolic Safety Data: Data on non-metabolic adverse events during each phase will be

725   Assumptions for Power Calculations for H1: We propose 20% as the minimal clinically meaningful
726   difference in relapse rates between olanzapine and placebo over 36 weeks. A 20% difference would mean that
727   5 patients (NNT) would need to be treated with olanzapine to prevent 1 case of relapse. Based on the review
728   of literature (Section 3.4) and STOP-PD stabilization data (Section 3.8), we estimate that 15% of participants
729   who are maintained on sertraline+olanzapine will have a relapse in the RCT. A relapse rate of 35% in the
730   sertraline+placebo group would therefore be consistent with the hypothesized 20% difference between
731   treatment groups. A 35% relapse following olanzapine discontinuation is the same as the rate reported by
732   Aronson et al.32 in a naturalistic study of antipsychotic discontinuation in PD, but not as high as some of the
733   rates of relapse of PD following cessation of a course of ECT.4,37-39 Based on our previous experience of
734   conducting studies of the continuation and maintenance treatment of PD,38,41,139 we predict that attrition during
735   the RCT will not exceed 10%.

760   3.10.5 Strategies for Attrition: Attrition can introduce bias and reduce power, precision and generalizability.148
761   Based on our prior studies, we expect no more than 10% attrition during the RCT (see above). Cox models
762   assume non-informative censoring. The assessment frequency allows us to gauge survival status no more
763   than 2 weeks prior to dropout. Also, for the survival analysis of H1, we will adhere to the Intent-to-Treat
764   principle, in that we will make every effort to continue assessments for the entire course of randomized
765   treatment or until relapse (which ever comes first), even among those nonadherent to randomized
766   assignment.149,150 Mixed models (H2, H3, E1, E2) yield valid inferences assuming ignorable attrition (i.e.
767   attrition is accounted for by covariates/dependent variable measured prior to drop-out).151 Following the
768   regulatory approach to safety, we will classify subjects based on medications taken during protocolized
769   treatment. We will examine the ignorable attrition assumption. First, using a pattern mixture model, 152
770   implemented in a longitudinal framework.153 Second, Intent-to-Attend at entry to the RCT will be used as a
771   covariate to account for attrition.154 Estimates of treatment effect from the models described above will be
772   compared with models that also include the main effects of either dropout pattern or Intent-to-Attend.

1067   Rationale for inclusion and exclusion criteria
1068    1) Age criterion: Excluding children below the age of 18 years from this study of PD is justified on ethical and
1069   scientific grounds. We do not know enough about the efficacy of these medications or their tolerability in
1070   children at target doses to justify including children.
1071
1072   2) Excluding patients with bipolar affective disorder: Persons with bipolar affective disorder require
1073   continuation and maintenance treatment with a mood-stabilizing medication to lessen the risk of relapse or
1074   recurrence of mania, hypomania, or depression. Antidepressant medication alone is not recommended in the
1075   continuation or maintenance treatment of bipolar disorder. As a result, we exclude patients with a known
1076   history of bipolar disorder, because it would not be ethical to discontinue olanzapine, which has efficacy as a
1077   mood-stabilizer in bipolar disorder, in these individuals.
1078
1079   3) Depression severity criteria: Only subjects with moderately severe to severe depression will be included to
1080   increase the likelihood that delusions are a symptom of PD, rather than of schizophrenia or another psychotic
1081   disorder with secondary depression.
1082
1083   4) Requiring delusions, with or without hallucinations: The rationale for requiring delusions, whether or not
1084   hallucinations are present, is based on the infrequency of hallucinations alone (that is, without delusions) in
1085   PD.158,159 Psychotic patients with hallucinations only, who do not have delusions, are more likely to be suffering
1086   from another disorder, such as schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, brief psychotic disorder, or a toxic-
1087   metabolic encephalopathy, as a cause of their psychotic symptoms. Thus, the requirement of delusions will
1088   facilitate diagnostic precision.
1089
                                                                                                                         22
1090   5) Exclusion of recent substance abuse or dependence: The DSM-IV criteria for substance abuse or
1091   dependence defines these disorders as a maladaptive pattern of substance use occurring within a 12-month
1092   period. We elected to use a narrower exclusion criterion of abuse (within the past 3 months) in order to allow
1093   participation by subjects with histories of substance abuse or dependence problems that could be secondary to
1094   their mood disorders. However, we decided to exclude individuals in whom very recent substance abuse or
1095   dependence might be contributing to the mood or psychotic symptoms. A valid and reliable diagnostic
1096   distinction between unipolar PD and a substance-induced mood disorder with psychotic features can not be
1097   made in the presence of current substance abuse. Participants with a secondary psychotic depression may
1098   have a different treatment response and longer term outcome than those with primary PD. However, allowing
1099   participation by patients with histories of substance abuse or dependence more than three months earlier
1100   broadens the inclusion criteria to more closely approximate patients seen in ‘real world’ settings.
1101
1102   6) Patients with schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, and other psychotic disorders are excluded, because
1103   the focus of this study is on major depressive disorder with psychotic features, not ‘primary’ psychotic disorders
1104   with associated depression. Current body dysmorphic disorder and obsessive-compulsive disorder are
1105   excluded because the ideation and phenomenology associated with these disorders may be difficult to reliably
1106   distinguish from PD.
1107   7) Persons with dementia are excluded for the following reasons:
1108       a)delusions and depression may be symptomatic manifestations of dementia. The pathophysiology,
1109       treatment response, and course of these symptoms of dementia may differ from those of PD.160 Moreover,
1110       non-delusional confabulations are a frequent symptom of dementia and may be difficult to reliably
1111       distinguish from delusions;
1112       b) dementia may affect a person’s ability to reliably and accurately report the presence and course of
1113       symptoms, which could affect the validity of ratings of outcome.
1114
1115   8) Persons with Type 1 diabetes mellitus are at risk of ketoacidosis if they become hyperglycaemic, whereas
1116   ketoacidosis is a very rare event in persons with Type 2 diabetes mellitus.157 Therefore, to avoid the potential
1117   risk of ketoacidosis precipitated by olanzapine-associated hyperglycemia, persons with Type I diabetes will be
1118   excluded from the study. In STOP-PD, no more than 7 of the 259 participants had Type 1 diabetes; therefore,
1119   we do not anticipate that this exclusion will have a significant impact on recruitment, the aims pertaining to
1120   metabolic changes, or generalizability of the study’s findings.
1121
1122   9) Acute or unstable medical illnesses may prevent participants from tolerating the acute trial target doses of
1123   study medications, interfere with subjects’ ability to complete the study, and/or contribute to psychiatric
1124   symptomatology. We will, however, include subjects with stable chronic physical illness, to examine the
1125   interaction between chronic medical burden, duration of treatment with olanzapine, and risk of
1126   relapse/recurrence.
1127
1128   10) Exclusion of women who are pregnant, plan to become pregnant or are breast-feeding. Sertraline and
1129   olanzapine are not specifically known to harm the fetus, but the data are limited. As a result, to err on the side
1130   of caution, pregnancy is an exclusion criterion. Sertraline is present in breast milk and, therefore, may be
1131   transmitted to breast-feeding infants.

1134   olanzapine during the index episode is justified on ethical and scientific grounds. It would not be ethical to

1159   4.2.3 Potential Risks: The most common side effects associated with the use of sertraline and olanzapine in
1160   STOP-PD (occurring in more than 10% of participants) were: weight gain, somnolence/sedation, gastrointestinal
1161   symptoms, experiencing at least one fall, and orthostatic dizziness. In addition, elevated levels of blood glucose,
1162   cholesterol and triglycerides have been associated with olanzapine. Olanzapine, along with other atypical
1163   antipsychotics, has been associated with a slight increased absolute risk of cerebrovascular events (mainly
1164   TIAs) in persons with severe dementia. However, we will not be including persons with dementia in this study.
1165   Users of antipsychotic drugs have an increased risk of mortality compared with non-users of this class of
1166   drugs: in the case of users of olanzapine, the increased absolute risk across diagnostic groups has been
1167   estimated at 0.9% over 2-3 years of current use.162 We will explicitly discuss the potential mortality risk in the
1168   consent process. Blood tests may cause some minor discomfort or bruising.
1169
1170   Severe depression increases the risk of suicidal ideation and behavior. Participation in the study will likely reduce
1171   this risk, but this is not guaranteed. The FDA recommends close observation of people taking antidepressant
1172   medication, including sertraline, for worsening depression or the emergence of suicidal thoughts or plans. As
1173   described in Section 3.9.5 participants will be closely monitored for suicidal ideation and behavior.
1174
1175   Discontinuation of antipsychotic medication in PD may possibly be associated with an increased risk of relapse
1176   and recurrence of PD, although this is not known. We have estimated the overall risk of relapse to be
1177   approximately 25% during the course of the RCT. Cases of relapse will be treated promptly.
1178
1179   Alternative Treatments: ECT can be used to successfully treat PD. However, ECT is associated with its own risks,
1180   including cognitive impairment, cardiac arrythmias, musculoskeletal injury, damage to teeth, and very small risk of
1181   death. Although ECT is a successful acute treatment for PD, it is associated with up to a 50% risk of relapse of PD
1182   in the 6 months following discontinuation of ECT. Antidepressant medications other than sertraline and
1183   antipsychotic medications other than olanzapine are used in clinical practice to treat PD. However, as discussed in
1184   Section 3.5, olanzapine is the only atypical antipsychotic with evidence of efficacy in the treatment of PD.

1258   worsening will be operationalized as a CGI change score of 6 or 7 (‘much worse’ or ‘very much worse’).

1261   research assessments on a weekly basis during the first 8 weeks of the RCT. These weekly assessments will
1262   cover the period of olanzapine taper and its immediate aftermath, thereby providing intensive follow-up of
1263   patients during this period. In addition, for the remainder of the RCT, participants will be assessed every 2
1264   weeks (alternating research and clinical assessments) to monitor for the possibility of clinical worsening. If a

1278   High risk of suicide is operationalized as any one of the following: 1) a current suicide plan; 2) a suicide attempt

1283   Stabilization Phase. If a participant has a current suicide plan or has had a current suicide attempt, he/she will
1284   be removed from the study and the research psychiatrist will make a clinical decision about the most
1285   appropriate course of action, including whether the patient requires immediate hospitalization. In the case of
1286   participants who are found to have a current suicide plan or had a current suicide attempt based on the
1287   research associate’s clinical phone assessment, the research psychiatrist, who will be available by pager, will
1288   be immediately informed by the research associate and the psychiatrist will then talk on the phone with the
1289   participant. The psychiatrist will then make a decision about whether the patient should be referred for
1290   immediate psychiatric hospitalization or whether it is safe for the participant to be seen for a research
1291   termination visit, and subsequent clinical management, the following business day.

1345   4.4 POTENTIAL BENEFITS
1346
1347    The benefits to participants will include:
1348   1. Evaluation of their mental and physical health;
1349   2. Ongoing monitoring of their clinical condition;
1350   3. Treatment with a medication regimen that has been shown to be efficacious in the acute treatment of PD;
1351   4. The possibility that symptoms of PD will improve with treatment.
1352
1353   The potential benefit to society is determining whether continued antipsychotic medication prevents relapse of
1354   PD.
1355
1356   4.5 IMPORTANCE OF KNOWLEDGE TO BE GAINED
1357
1358   It is not known whether antipsychotic medication needs to be continued once an episode of PD has responded
1359   to combined antidepressant-antipsychotic treatment. This issue is of tremendous clinical importance. On the
1360   one hand, the unnecessary continuation of antipsychotic medication exposes a patient to adverse effects,
1361   some of which have considerable public health significance. On the other hand, premature discontinuation of
1362   antipsychotic medication has the potential risk of early relapse of a severe, disabling disorder. Addressing
1363   these issues will be of major public health importance. In our view, given the precautions that will be taken in
1364   this study, the likely benefits to participants and society outweight the potential risks.

1965   A1. ACUTE PHASE
1966
1967   After written consent is obtained, psychotropic medications that are not permitted in the study (Section 4.2.1)
1968   will be withdrawn over a period of up to 4 days, when feasible, prior to starting study medications. However,
1969   withdrawal of anticonvulsant medications with mood-stabilizing properties that are not permitted in the study
1970   will be extended to 10 days, and may therefore include the first week of the acute treatment phase. In addition,
1971   if a participant experiences withdrawal symptoms associated with the taper of psychotropic medication during
1972   the 4-day period, the taper may be slowed and continued over 10 days, which will overlap with the first week of
1973   the acute phase treatment. Since one of the goals of the acute phase is to maximize the recruitment of
1974   participants to the RCT, it is important to allow flexibility in the rate of withdrawal of preexisting psychotropic
1975   medications, to maximize the chance of recruiting and retaining participants. Persons who are taking sertraline,
1976   olanzapine, or both medications at the time of signing consent for the acute phase of treatment, where this
1977   does not affect eligibility for the study (Section 4.2.1), will remain on these medications, and upon entry to the
1978   study will have the existing dosages adjusted, when necessary, to reach target doses, based on the protocol’s
1979   titration schedule. The only psychotropic medications allowed in the study will be olanzapine, sertraline, and
1980   ‘as needed’ lorazepam; ‘as needed’ benztropine will be allowed to treat EPSE (see below).
1981
1982   Sertraline and olanzapine will be dispensed in a non-blind, open-label fashion during the acute and
1983   stabilization phases of the study. Sertraline will be dispensed in the morning as 50 mg tablets. Olanzapine will
1984   be dispensed in the evening as 5 mg tablets. The titration schedule of sertraline and olanzapine, and the target
1985   dosages of these medications, will be the same as that successfully employed in STOP-PD. Thus, sertraline
1986   will be started at a dosage of 50 mg/day and olanzapine will be started at a dosage of 5 mg/day. On day 4, the
1987   dosages of sertraline and olanzapine will be increased to 100 mg/day and 10 mg/day, respectively, as
1988   tolerated. On day 7, the dosages of sertraline and olanzapine will be increased to the target doses of 150
1989   mg/day and 15 mg/day, respectively, as tolerated. If a participant experiences side effects during the titration
1990   phase, downward titration of one or both medications will be allowed as indicated, and the titration of
1991   medications can proceed more slowly. In these cases, however, the goal of titration will be to reach target
1992   doses within 14 days of starting the medications.
1993
1994   A1.1 Dosage Adjustments Due to Inadequate Improvement: If there is no improvement or inadequate
1995   improvement in psychosis, based on the research psychiatrist’s clinical judgment, after 7 days of 15 mg/day of
1996   olanzapine, the dosage of olanzapine will be increased to 20 mg/day, as tolerated. This flexibility in dosing will
1997   allow for dosages to be used at the maximum dose recommended in the product monograph by 2 weeks of
1998   starting treatment. Participants who did not experience at least partial improvement in depressive symptoms
1999   (partial improvement defined as a 17-item HAM-D score of below 16) after taking sertraline at a dosage of 150
2000   mg/day for 4 weeks, will have the sertraline dose increased to 200 mg/day, as tolerated.
2001
2002   A1.2 Dosage Adjustments Due to Side Effects: The treating psychiatrist will be allowed to adjust the titration
2003   of sertraline, olanzapine, or both if the participant experiences side effects that are troublesome or otherwise
2004   clinically significant (e.g., sedation, orthostatic hypertension). In such cases, the titration of both sertraline and
2005   olanzapine will be halted, or if clinically indicated, the dosage of one or both medications can be reduced.
2006   However, titration to reach the target dose will resume, if and when the side effects resolve. If, after a further
2007   attempt at titration, a participant is unable to tolerate 150 mg/day of sertraline or 15 mg/day of olanzapine, the


                                                                                                                          39
2008   participant may be maintained on a lower dose of these medications, with a minimum allowable dose of 50
2009   mg/day of sertraline and 5 mg/day of olanzapine.
2010
2011   A1.3 Rationale for Dosing: In STOP-PD, by the end of the second week of treatment, the average daily dose
2012   of sertraline was 150 mg/ day, for both younger and older age groups, and the average daily dose of
2013   olanzapine was 14 mg in the younger group and 13 mg in the older group. These data indicate the feasibility
2014   of aiming for target dosages of 150 mg/day of sertraline and 15 mg/day of olanzapine in both age groups. The
2015   target dosages of sertraline and olanzapine are designed to ensure that participants receive therapeutic
2016   dosages of these medications early in treatment, in an attempt to facilitate improvement as rapidly as possible.
2017   Although sertraline has a flat dose-response curve (Preskorn 1993), meaning that, at a group level, higher
2018   doses are not necessarily associated with a higher frequency of response, there are individuals who do require
2019   higher doses in order to respond. Thus, in order to maximize the chance of early response in all participants,
2020   we elected to increase the dose of sertraline to 150mg/day early in treatment, rather than maintain the dose at
2021   a lower level for several weeks, before increasing the dose in non-responders.
2022
2023   Many persons with PD are hospitalized, they are frequently severely distressed by their delusions, and food
2024   and fluid intake may be compromised by their psychosis. As a result, it is imperative to treat the psychosis in
2025   an expeditious manner. Thus, in patients who fail to show significant improvement in psychosis by the end of 7
2026   days of treatment with 15 mg/day of olanzapine, the dosage will be increased to 20 mg/day, as tolerated.
2027
2028   A1.4 As-Needed Lorazepam: Persons with severe anxiety, restlessness, or insomnia that would prevent them
2029   from continuing in the study can be prescribed lorazepam, starting at 0.5 to 2 mg/day, as needed. A maximum
2030   daily dosage of 3 mg/day will be allowed during the study, but doses will be kept at a minimum and the
2031   lorazepam will be withdrawn as early as possible.
2032
2033   A1.5 As-Needed Benztropine: Among participants who develop clinically significant EPSE, the dose of
2034   olanzapine will be reduced by 5 mg increments, to a minimum dose of 10 mg/day, if needed to lessen
2035   extrapyramidal effects. However, if problematic EPSE persist, despite the dose reduction of olanzapine,
2036   benztropine can be prescribed, to a maximum dose of 2 mg/day. Lorazepam and benztropine will not be
2037   administered within the 12 hours prior to administration of cognitive tests, so as not to disrupt cognitive
2038   function.The prescription of benztropine will be taken into account in the secondary statistical analyses of
2039   EPSE.

2041   A2. STABILIZATION PHASE
2042
2043   When participants meet remission criteria, they will continue with open-label sertraline and olanzapine during
2044   the 8-week stabilization phase. Since the goal of this phase is to consolidate stability of remission, adjustment
2045   of the doses of study medications will be allowed, if necessitated by clinical worsening or significant side
2046   effects. In the case of exacerbation of depressive symptoms, sertraline can be increased as tolerated by 50mg
2047   increments every 7 days, to the maximum dose of 200mg/day. Because sustained remission of psychosis
2048   during the stabilization phase will be required for eligibility for the RCT (Section 3.9.2), participants will leave
2049   the stabilization phase if they experience a relapse of delusions or hallucinations as determined by SCID
2050   interview. Nevertheless, in the case of participants who experience worsening of worry that is not delusional,
2051   olanzapine can be increased as tolerated by 5 mg increments every 7 days, to the maximum dose of
2052   20mg/day. If dosage reductions are necessitated by side effects, sertraline can be decreased by 50 mg
2053   increments every 7 days as needed, to a minimum dose of 50 mg/day, and olanzapine can be decreased by
2054   5mg increments every 7 days as needed, to a minimum dose of 5 mg/day.

2056   A3. RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIAL
2057
2058   Participants who meet the study’s remission criteria, will be randomly allocated, following completion of the
2059   stabilization phase, to either continue olanzapine or switch from olanzapine to placebo (see Data Analysis
2060   section for details of the randomization procedure). Randomization assignments will be generated by Dr. Andy
2061   Leon, the study’s statistician, and sent to the research pharmacist at each site who will dispense study
2062   medications. Records of each subject’s treatment assignment will be kept at the respective pharmacies and

                                                                                                                         40
2063   made available if a clinical emergency occurs. Site principal investigators and their co-investigators will carry
2064   beepers to ensure that 24-hour/day coverage is provided for medical emergencies that may arise in study
2065   participants.

2067   A3.1 Sertraline: Participants will take open-label sertraline for the duration of the RCT. The goal will be to
2068   maintain sertraline at the same acute dose that the participant was prescribed at the time of randomization to
2069   the RCT. Because participants will have been taking sertraline for a minimum of 10 weeks and a maximum of
2070   20 weeks before entering the RCT, we anticipate that the dose of sertraline will rarely need to be adjusted in
2071   the RCT because of side effects. However, if for any reason, side effects do necessitate a dose reduction, the
2072   reason for contemplating a dose reduction will need to be discussed with the site’s principal investigator, to
2073   ensure that this is the most appropriate course of action. If a dose reduction is agreed upon, the dose can be
2074   reduced by 50 mg increments every 7 days, to a minimum dose of 50 mg/day, if required. An alternative
2075   approach to side effect burden during the RCT would be to disallow any reduction in the dose of sertraline, so
2076   as not to confound the analyses of relapse and recurrence. However, forcing a participant to leave the RCT
2077   because of intolerable side effects, when a dose reduction may alleviate the problem and allow the participant
2078   to continue, does not reflect ‘real world’ clinical practice, and would potentially weaken the power of the
2079   statistical models through forced attrition. An increase in dose of sertraline will not be permitted at any time
2080   during the RCT, because it will confound testing of H1. Dose changes of sertraline will be carefully recorded
2081   and will be reported in a descriptive fashion for each treatment arm.

2094   A3.3 Dose Adjustments of Olanzapine/Placebo During the RCT: The goal will be to maintain
2095   olanzapine/placebo at the same acute dose that the participant was prescribed at the time of randomization to
2096   the RCT. However, a change in dose of olanzapine/placebo will be permitted, if necessitated by adverse
2097   effects or clinical worsening during the RCT. The rationale for allowing a change in olanzapine/placebo dose, is
2098   that the primary aim of the study is to assess whether olanzapine, administered within a clinically relevant dose
2099   range, prevents relapse, not whether a specific dose of olanzapine prevents relapse. Moreover, permitting a
2100   dose adjustment in response to side effects or symptoms of the illness reflects ‘real world’ practice and
2101   increases the likelihood of retention of participants in the study. If a change in dose of olanzapine/placebo is
2102   indicated, the daily dose can be adjusted by 1 tablet every 7 days, as needed, within the dose range of 5-20
2103   mg/day of olanzapine/placebo. The reason(s) for each change of dose will need to be discussed with the site’s
2104   PI and will be documented in the case record form. Dose changes of olanzapine/placebo will be carefully
2105   recorded and will be reported in a descriptive fashion for each treatment arm.
